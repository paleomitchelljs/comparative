# Werneburg & Maier (2019) — Diverging development of akinetic skulls in turtles

## Citation

Werneburg I, Maier W. 2019. Diverging development of akinetic skulls in cryptodire
and pleurodire turtles: an ontogenetic and phylogenetic study. *Vertebrate Zoology*
69(2): 113–143.

## Question

Extant turtles have an **akinetic** skull; early turtles (Testudinata) still had
kinetic ones. How was the akinetic condition built, and did the two modern clades
build it the same way?

## Findings worth carrying

**They did not.** Pleurodira and Cryptodira arrive at akinesis by strikingly
different developmental routes in the orbitotemporal and quadrate regions. In one,
the ascending process of the palatoquadrate — later ossified as the epipterygoid —
does the work of forming the secondary lateral braincase wall; the other clade
solves it otherwise.

Four character complexes are analysed: snout stiffening, reduction of the
basipterygoid process, formation of a secondary lateral braincase wall, and fusion
of the palatoquadrate cartilage to the braincase.

**Why that matters for muscles.** The adductor chamber is bounded by exactly these
structures, so a jaw muscle's origin in a turtle is on a wall whose developmental
history differs between the two clades. Two turtles with the same muscle on the same
named bone did not necessarily get there the same way, and this is the source that
says so.

## Scored

1 occurrence row on `testudines-generalised` — `adductor-mandibulae-posterior`, one
to two heads arising anteromedially on the quadrate, medial to the adductor
mandibulae externus, inserting partly directly and partly by its own tendon.

Its insertion on the articular puts it at the centre of the inverted U formed by the
other components, which is Johnston's (2011) criterion for partitioning the adductor
complex.

## Limitations

- **Developmental and phylogenetic, not descriptive.** 24 origin/insertion mentions,
  and one scorable row.
- **`testudines-generalised`**, like the other turtle cranial sources — no single
  animal is described.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The right paper for showing that a shared adult condition can have two developmental
histories, which is the strongest available caution against reading homology off
adult topology alone. Pair with **Werneburg (2011)** for the same author's turtle
cranial nomenclature and with **Johnston (2011, 2014)**, where attachment beats
innervation as the homology criterion in the jaw.

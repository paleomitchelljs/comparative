# Sefton, Bhullar, Mohaddes & Hanken 2016 — Head-trunk interface in tetrapods

## Citation

Sefton, E. M., Bhullar, B.-A. S., Mohaddes, Z., & Hanken, J. (2016). Evolution of the head-trunk interface in tetrapod vertebrates. *eLife* 5:e09972. DOI 10.7554/eLife.09972.

Harvard (Hanken lab, MCZ) + Yale (Bhullar) collaboration. First author Elizabeth Sefton. Published 19 April 2016.

## Question

> "The extent to which the cucullaris muscle is a cranial muscle allied with the gill levators of anamniotes or is instead a trunk muscle is an ongoing debate."

Two related but separable questions:

1. **Phylogenetic distribution of the cucullaris**: does it exist in coelacanth and caecilians (taxa where it was reported absent)? Is it serially homologous with the gill levators across gnathostomes?
2. **Embryonic origin of the cucullaris**: is it branchiomeric cranial muscle (like the gill levators) or is it trunk-derived (posterior lateral plate mesoderm of a trunk myogenic program)? And where does the head-trunk boundary lie — in the paraxial mesoderm, in the lateral plate mesoderm, and are the two congruent?

> "We show that unsegmented mesoderm adjacent to the anterior three somites contributes to the cucullaris as well as to the gill-levator muscles in a manner consistent with their apparent serial homology, which supports categorization of the cucullaris as a branchiomeric muscle."

## Time frame

Extant. Phylogenetic coverage spans crown Gnathostomata (chondrichthyans through mammals).

## Methods

- Lugol-stain (5% in 70% ethanol, 7–10 days) + XRA-002 microCT at Harvard Center for Nanoscale Systems; reconstructed in VGStudio Max 2.0. Eight figure supplements give stereo images of chimaera, bichir, lungfish, coelacanth, axolotl, caecilian, anole, and opossum.
- Orthotopic + heterotopic grafting of GFP-labeled unsegmented cranial mesoderm (stages 18–22) from donor into white mutant hosts; readout via live fluorescence + anti-GFP IHC + 12/101 (muscle) + desmin + acetylated α-tubulin (axons) + DAPI. Optical projection tomography (OPT) at University of Washington.
- RNA in situ hybridization with antisense riboprobes for *lhx2*, *msc*, *pitx2*, *tbx1* (primer sequences in Table 1). In albino *A. mexicanum* embryos.

## Findings

1. **The cucullaris exists in every gnathostome clade examined by μCT — including coelacanth and caecilians, where it was previously reported absent.**

   > "The cucullaris has not been described in the musculoskeletally conservative coelacanth, nor in the limbless caecilian amphibians. Based on novel dissections, Greenwood and Lauder (1981) reported the cucullaris absent from the coelacanth. Millot and Anthony (1958), however, had earlier briefly described in the coelacanth a fifth gill levator that originates on the anocleithrum of the pectoral girdle... We examined this muscle in both a CT scan of a contrast-stained coelacanth pup and an MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) scan of an adult. The muscle is also larger than previously described, with several heads originating on the pectoral girdle (Figure 1C). It is angled differently from the other levators, but its fibers remain in close association with them and extend from the anocleithrum to insert on the fifth ceratobranchial."

   The coelacanth thus retains the plesiomorphic tripartite cucullaris attachment: origin on the pectoral girdle (anocleithrum), insertion on the posterior cranial skeleton, insertion on the fifth ceratobranchial (Fig. 6C–E). Caecilian m. levator arcus branchiales complex in *Typhlonectes natans* — a triangular structure from otic capsule + dorsal trunk-muscle fascia → posteriormost ceratobranchial — has a posterior division (pars posterosuperficialis, Wilkinson & Nussbaum 1997) insertinng on the fascia separating rectus abdominis from interhyoideus. Based on topology, **the posterior division of the m. levator arcus branchiales complex in caecilians = the cucullaris homolog**, connecting the otic capsule (rather than a pectoral girdle, which caecilians have lost) with ventral trunk fascia.

2. **Cross-gnathostome morphology (Fig. 1).**
   - *Hydrolagus* (chimaera): massive cucullaris that may incorporate anterior gill levators; its boundary with the gill-levator series is not obviously distinct. Pair with Anderson 2008 for a matching cranial-muscle-homology view.
   - *Polypterus*: thin strap-like "protractor pectoralis" (Greenwood & Lauder 1981) — a cucullaris continuous with the anterior gill levators, shares muscle fibers with the immediately anterior levator, originates from posterior head and inserts on pectoral girdle and (when present) fifth gill arch.
   - *Protopterus* (lungfish): retains the tripartite ancestral attachment — origin on pectoral girdle + insertion on posterior skull + insertion on fifth ceratobranchial (Fig. 6C–E).
   - *Latimeria*: the "fifth gill levator" of Millot & Anthony 1958, enlarged on CT/MRI, is the cucullaris homolog.
   - *Typhlonectes*: cucullaris homolog = posterior division of m. levator arcus branchiales complex.
   - *Anolis* + *Monodelphis*: large wedge-shaped cucullaris ("trapezius"), the primitive amniote condition.

3. **Cucullaris develops from unsegmented cranial mesoderm adjacent to somites 1–3 in the axolotl.**

   GFP+ mesoderm lateral to somites 1–3 was grafted at stage 21 into d/d hosts and traced through stage 57.

   > "By stage 35, GFP+ cells were visible dorsal to the developing gill buds in the region of the presumptive gill muscles, the cucullaris and the dilatator laryngis (Figure 2D–G). In cross section, labeled cells were present throughout the length of the cucullaris but absent from the somitic hypobranchial or epaxial muscles (Figure 2H,I), thus indicating little if any somitic contamination."

   Three regions of cranial mesoderm anterior to somite 1 give rise to the first gill-levator muscle via unsegmented mesoderm just anterior to somite 1; the posterior three gill levators come from unsegmented mesoderm at the level of somites 1–3 (Fig. 2—figure supplement 3). **Posterior limit of cranial mesoderm in the axolotl is at the axial level of somite 3.** Axolotl mesoderm adjacent to somites 1–3 adjacent to the head-trunk boundary, which Brazeau et al. 2023 now argue corresponds to the soft-tissue trace of the (lost) endoskeletal sixth branchial arch.

4. **Cranial muscle regulatory network genes mark the cucullaris / gill-levator region.**
   - *islet1* is expressed in anterior cranial mesoderm associated with the second heart field (stage 34 onward); expands dorsally over the gill region (stage 36); by stage 36–40 forms stripes dorsal to the gills including the levator anlage + cucullaris (Fig. 2J–M′).
   - *tbx1* also expressed in the developing gill muscle region at stages 35 + 38.
   - *msc* expressed in the gill region by stage 38. *tbx1* + *msc* extend distally into the external gills; *isl1* does not.
   - The axolotl cucullaris therefore develops through the canonical cranial-muscle regulatory program (Sambasivan et al. 2009; Lescroart et al. 2015).
   - The "mouse laryngeal-muscle + trapezius clonal-common-origin" finding of Lescroart et al. 2015 is directly supported in the axolotl: laryngeal muscle derivatives in axolotl trace back to the same somite-adjacent cranial mesoderm that generates the cucullaris.

5. **Molecular regionalization of mandibular + hyoid arch muscles in axolotl.**
   - Mandibular adductors levator mandibulae externus (lme) + l.m. anterior (lma / pseudotemporalis) develop within the mandibular arch. *lhx2* is expressed in the mesodermal core of the pharyngeal arches at stage 34; by stage 40 restricted to specific muscle groups including lme + ventral mandibular arch musculature; not in lma (Fig. 3).
   - *isl1* expressed at stage 40 in lma (superficial anterior adductor) and in trigeminal nerve.
   - *pitx2* expressed in hyoid arch mesoderm + migrating hypobranchial muscle precursors by stage 34; concentrated in hyoid musculature at stage 40 (depressor mandibulae, branchiohyoideus externus — Figs. 4E,F); **not in gill musculature**. Interesting from a conservation perspective: in the mouse, *pitx2* is required to specify mesoderm of the mandibular arch but not the hyoid arch (Shih et al. 2007a,b); in the axolotl, the opposite regional pattern is seen. This is a reshuffling of cranial-muscle genetic-program assignments, not a strict 1:1 conservation.
   - Together, differential *isl1* / *tbx1* / *msc* / *lhx2* / *pitx2* expression demonstrates that the axolotl cranial mesoderm is molecularly regionalized even within individual pharyngeal arches and even within the mandibular adductor complex — supporting genetic heterogeneity of cranial muscle development established for amniotes (Nathan et al. 2008; Dong et al. 2006; Kelly et al. 2004; Marcucio & Noden 1999).

6. **Heterotopic transplants: lateral plate mesoderm from the cucullaris region can be re-patterned to mandibular or hyoid arch muscle.**
   - GFP+ LPM from the cucullaris region (adjacent to somite 2) transplanted anteriorly into mandibular arch or hyoid arch mesoderm integrated into both dorsal and ventral mandibular arch or hyoid arch muscle (Fig. 5B,C″). Transplanted cells were innervated normally by trigeminal (for mandibular) or facial (for hyoid) nerve. **Local cues are sufficient to re-pattern myogenic LPM from the cucullaris region.**
   - More posterior LPM (adjacent to somite 5) transplanted to mandibular arch mesoderm did **not** form muscle in 9 of 10 larvae (Fig. 5D,E). **Neither mandibular nor hyoid arch mesoderm cues are sufficient to turn posterior LPM myogenic.**
   - Conclusion: the cucullaris's LPM-adjacent mesoderm is **cranial mesoderm** up to somite 3, not "posterior LPM" in a trunk-myogenic sense.

7. **Head-trunk boundary congruence (Fig. 6).**
   - In the axolotl, the head-trunk boundary in unsegmented mesoderm is **at the same axial level as the somitic head-trunk boundary** (somite 3). Paraxial and lateral mesoderm anterior to somite 3 form cranial structures (including the heart).
   - In the chicken, the head-trunk boundary in somitic mesoderm is posterior to that in unsegmented mesoderm (Couly et al. 1993; Piekarski & Olsson 2014; Huang et al. 2000). Amniote LPM extends further caudally into cranial territory than somitic mesoderm does.
   - **The axolotl pattern (congruent boundaries) is plausibly plesiomorphic for tetrapods**, and the amniote pattern (offset boundaries) is derived. This suggests that mammalian + avian trapezius LPM-origin fate-mapping (Theis et al. 2010; Lescroart et al. 2015; Matsuoka et al. 2005) reflects a derived caudal extension of cranial-program-competent mesoderm into territory that in anamniotes would be occupied by somitic material.
   - Open question: does somite plasticity differ from LPM plasticity? Heterotopic transplantations suggest yes — somites that contribute to posterior skull are able to generate vertebrae when moved caudally (Kant & Goldstein 1999), but posterior cranial mesoderm that generates the cucullaris cannot generate muscle when moved caudally (Theis et al. 2010). Cranial mesoderm may have a tighter regional-fate constraint than somitic mesoderm at the head-trunk boundary.

## Limitations

- Axolotl-centric. Only one amphibian fate-mapped; the "plesiomorphic" congruent-boundary interpretation rests on this single exemplar + the chicken contrast.
- *Tbx1*, *msc*, *islet1*, *pitx2*, *lhx2* — five genes; many more in the cranial-muscle regulatory network. Molecular regionalization story is indicative, not exhaustive.
- Caecilian cucullaris identification relies on topological inference (posterior division of m. levator arcus branchiales complex inserting on ventral trunk fascia) — no developmental or lineage-tracing work in caecilians to confirm.
- The coelacanth "fifth gill levator" was re-interpreted as cucullaris based on μCT + MRI morphology — no developmental access possible.
- Heterotopic-transplant sample sizes small (9 of 10 failures for somite-5 LPM → mandibular).
- Authors acknowledge: "It is unclear if a portion of the anterior division should also be considered part of the cucullaris, connecting to the adult hyobranchial skeleton. In the former case, this unusual configuration might express an ancestral potential of the cucullaris to attach to the gill skeleton, and it evokes reports that the paired fin/limb apparatus has surprising developmental resemblance to the gill arches (Gillis et al., 2009)." — deep homology hint but not tested here.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

This paper is the modern μCT + fate-mapping + molecular-regionalization case for **the cucullaris as the branchiomeric/cranial muscle that spans the head-trunk boundary** — the soft-tissue thread connecting the vertebrate head and shoulder through deep time. Three teaching uses:

- **Direct pair with Brazeau et al. 2023**: Brazeau argues the endoskeletal pectoral girdle derives from the lost sixth branchial arch, and names the cucullaris as the soft-tissue trace of that arch. Sefton et al. 2016 provides the developmental evidence Brazeau's hypothesis needs — cucullaris is serially in the gill-levator series morphologically (Fig. 1, every gnathostome); develops from cranial mesoderm at the axolotl head-trunk boundary (Fig. 2 + 6); uses the cranial muscle regulatory program (*isl1* + *tbx1* + *msc*); is re-patternable as mandibular or hyoid muscle by local cues (Fig. 5); is congruent with the head-trunk boundary in the amphibian plesiomorphic pattern (Fig. 6). Sefton 2016 + Brazeau 2023 make the single strongest recent paired-paper argument for **"the shoulder is part of the head"** as a teachable headline.

- **"The cucullaris is there in taxa that were said to lack it"** — the coelacanth + caecilian findings are good pedagogically because they use μCT to overturn older dissection-based absence claims. Fig. 1 shows it in six gnathostomes on one page. Pair with Anderson 2008 (cranial muscle homology across gnathostomes, which puts the cucullaris in the holocephalan + elasmobranch context) and Ziermann & Diogo 2013 (axolotl cranial muscle development atlas).

- **Genetic regionalization within a single pharyngeal arch** — the *lhx2* / *isl1* split within the mandibular adductor complex + the *pitx2* hyoid-not-mandibular axolotl pattern give a concrete case for "even a single arch's muscles are mosaic at the level of gene expression," which connects to the Smith-Paredes et al. 2022 dorsal/ventral forelimb-mesenchyme-cleavage story and the Molnar et al. 2018 pectoral muscle homology story.

Pair with: Brazeau et al. 2023 (sixth-arch → pectoral girdle; cucullaris = soft-tissue trace); Ziermann & Diogo 2013 (axolotl cranial muscle development); Anderson 2008 (gnathostome cranial muscle homology); Gambaryan et al. 2015 (monotreme forelimb musculature continuity with neck muscles); Molnar et al. 2018 (fins-to-limbs pectoral muscle evolution); Lyson et al. 2013 (turtle nuchal bone + shoulder girdle reorganization); Ahlberg 2018 (tetrapod origin); Higashiyama et al. 2016 (vagus nerve + head-trunk boundary complement); Smith-Paredes et al. 2022 (amniote forelimb muscle cleavage — pair for conservation-of-developmental-pattern).

Best single teaching takeaway: the cucullaris/trapezius is the **one continuously-traceable soft-tissue element linking head and shoulder from chimaera to mammal** — a counterpoint to the textbook convenience of treating head and body as separate developmental modules.

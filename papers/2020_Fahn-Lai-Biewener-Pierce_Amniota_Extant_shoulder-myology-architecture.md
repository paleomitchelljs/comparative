# Fahn-Lai, Biewener & Pierce (2020) — Shoulder muscle architecture across two amniotes

## Citation

Fahn-Lai P, Biewener AA, Pierce SE. 2020. Broad similarities in shoulder muscle
architecture and organization across two amniotes: implications for reconstructing
non-mammalian synapsids. *PeerJ* 8: e8556.

## Question

Upright limb posture in mammals may have enabled the forelimb's diversification.
The non-mammalian synapsid fossil record holds the transition from sprawling to
erect limb function — but reconstructing posture from fossils needs a prior
understanding of muscle functional anatomy in living animals bracketing that
transition.

## Taxa and material

Gross morphology **and internal architecture** of the muscles crossing the shoulder
in two amniotes, a monotreme and a lepidosaur — the bracket a non-mammalian synapsid
sits inside.

## The finding

**Broad similarity**, which is the useful result and is in the title. Two animals of
very different posture share shoulder muscle architecture and organisation closely
enough that a synapsid reconstruction can lean on the shared condition rather than
having to choose between them.

## Scored

2 occurrence rows — `supracoracoideus` on *Tachyglossus aculeatus* and on *Galictis
cuja*.

**One of those rows carries a naming problem that this dataset has to hold open.**
The monotreme attachments are Gambaryan et al.'s *m. supracoracoideus*, which is the
muscle Diogo & Abdala (2010) call the **infraspinatus**. The two muscles this
record's synonym list treats as its mammalian halves are therefore not what a
monotreme paper means by the name. The note on the row carries the equivalence, and
nothing is renamed on one paper's say-so — see the Gambaryan reading note for the
three-way chain.

## Limitations

- **Two species.** A bracket, not a survey.
- **Architecture is the contribution.** The attachment rows here are thin, and the
  monotreme shoulder is scored from Gambaryan et al. rather than from this paper.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Good for the point that a fossil reconstruction is only as good as the extant
bracket, and that a bracket whose two halves *agree* is worth more than one whose
halves are each precisely described but different. Pair with **Gambaryan et al.
(2015)** and **Bishop & Pierce (2024)** on synapsid hindlimb biomechanics.

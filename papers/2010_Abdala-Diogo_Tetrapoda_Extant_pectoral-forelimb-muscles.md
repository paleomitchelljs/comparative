# Abdala & Diogo 2010 — Pectoral and forelimb muscles of limbed amphibians and reptiles

## Citation

Abdala, V., & Diogo, R. (2010). Comparative anatomy, homologies and evolution of the pectoral and forelimb musculature of tetrapods with special attention to extant limbed amphibians and reptiles. *Journal of Anatomy*, 217(5), 536–573. doi:10.1111/j.1469-7580.2010.01278.x

## Extracted quotes

### Question / scope
- "The main aim of the present work is to synthesize the information obtained from our dissections of the pectoral and forelimb muscles of representative members of the major extant taxa of limbed amphibians and reptiles and from our review of the literature, in order to provide an account of the comparative anatomy, homologies and evolution of these muscles in the Tetrapoda."
- Companion paper: "Diogo et al. (2009a) summarized the results of their long-term study... Of the seven sarcopterygian taxa featured in the tables of that paper, only two, the urodele *Ambystoma ordinarium* and the lepidosaur *Timon lepidus*, were non-mammalian tetrapods."
- Excluded: "caecilian amphibians, amphisbaenians, and snakes usually lack limbs, their pectoral and forelimb musculature being extremely reduced; these taxa will not be discussed in the present paper."

### Taxa dissected
- Amphibia: *Ambystoma mexicanum*, *A. ordinarium*, *A. texanum*, *Rhinella arenarum*, *Litoria caerulea*, *Phyllomedusa sauvagi*, *Telmatobius laticeps*.
- Aves: *Cairina moschata*, *Coturnix*, *Gallus domesticus*, *Nothura*, *Pitangus sulphuratus*, *Thraupis sayaca*.
- Crocodylia: *Caiman latirostris*.
- Lepidosauria: >50 species across *Ameiva*, *Amphisbaena*, *Anisolepis*, *Basiliscus*, *Callopistes*, *Chalcides*, *Cnemidophorus*, *Cordylus*, *Diplolaemus*, *Dracaena*, *Gekko*, *Hemidactylus*, *Leiosaurus*, *Liolaemus*, *Mabuya*, *Phelsuma*, *Phymaturus*, *Podarcis*, *Polychrus*, *Tropidurus*, *Sphenodon*, etc.
- Testudines: *Rhinoclemmys pulcherrima*, *Sacalia bealei*, *Terrapene carolina*, *Testudo graeca*, *Trachemys scripta*.

### Core findings
- "The pectoral and forelimb musculature of all these major taxa conform to a general pattern that seems to have been acquired very early in the evolutionary history of tetrapods. Although some muscles are missing in certain taxa, and a clear departure from this general pattern is obviously present in derived groups such as birds, the same overall configuration is easily distinguishable in these taxa."
- "In tetrapods, the muscles of the radial and ulnar complexes of the forearm are pleisomorphically mainly inserted onto the radius/ulna or onto the more proximal carpal bones, but in mammals some of these muscles insert more distally onto bones such as the metacarpals. Interestingly, a similar trend towards a more distal insertion of these muscles is also found in some non-mammalian tetrapod taxa, such as some anurans (e.g. *Phyllomedusa*). This may be correlated with the acquisition of more subtle digital movement abilities in these latter taxa."
- Holmes (1977) quote: "The evolution of the forearm musculature has been quite conservative in tetrapods and that the pectoral girdle and limb musculature of living reptiles of such divergent types of *Sphenodon*, *Iguana*, *Pseudemys*, and *Crocodylus* shows many features in common."

### Counter to "humans have special hands" narrative
- "In humans, the number of intrinsic muscles of the hand is actually smaller than that found in chimpanzees and numerous other primates, as well as in other tetrapods such as 'lizards' and urodeles; what is actually peculiar in humans is the great number of forearm muscles that attach directly on the digits, including muscles that are not differentiated in most other tetrapods and even in most other primates, such as the extensor pollicis brevis and the flexor pollicis longus."

### Position vs. identity of digits
- "The most radial digit of adult anurans corresponds to digit 2 of tetrapods with five digits... In anurans the probable homologue of the abductor pollicis longus goes to digit 2, and not to digit 1, as is often the case in other tetrapods. So, interestingly, in this specific case, what seems to be important for the formation and attachments of the abductor pollicis longus is mainly the position, and not the 'specific identity', of the digit to which the muscle attaches."
- "In urodeles such as *Taricha* and *Ambystoma*, the probable homologue of the abductor digiti minimi of other tetrapods... goes to digit 4 and not to digit 5."
- Counter case (identity-based): "In chickens... the most ulnar digit of adult chickens is digit 3 according to most studies of fossils and hox genes and digit 4 according to most embryological studies. So, in this case, it seems that the 'specific identity' of the digit is actually important."

### Head vs. limb patterning contrast
- "This contrasts with the patterning and development of the head muscles in tetrapods and other vertebrates, in which there is a highly constrained pattern of cranial skeletomuscular connectivity; each rhombomeric neural crest population remains coherent throughout ontogeny, forming both the connective tissues of specific muscles and their respective attachment sites. In the head there is a strong link between the insertions of the muscles and the identity of the specific neural crest population."

### Limitations
- "Further studies, ideally including a detailed analysis of the innervation and development of the 'rhomboideus' and the 'palmaris longus' of a broader sampling of amphibian and reptilian taxa, are needed to investigate whether these structures are homologous to the mammalian rhomboideus and palmaris longus, respectively."
- Bird digit identity (3 vs. 4 for most ulnar) remains unresolved between fossil+hox evidence and embryology.
- Caecilians, amphisbaenians, snakes excluded by scope.

## Summary

**Question.** Companion to Diogo et al. (2009a). That earlier paper covered sarcopterygian fish through humans with only two non-mammalian tetrapods (*Ambystoma* and *Timon*). This paper fills the gap by dissecting and homologizing pectoral and forelimb muscles across the major extant limbed amphibian and reptilian groups: urodeles, anurans, turtles, lepidosaurs, crocodilians, and birds.

**Taxa and material.** Dissections of about 150 specimens spanning 100+ species across all six target groups, plus literature synthesis. The most diverse sample is Lepidosauria (>50 squamate species plus *Sphenodon*). Caecilians, amphisbaenians, and snakes excluded because they are limbless.

**Time frame.** Extant.

**Methods.** Direct dissection + literature. Homology hypotheses framed as "secondary homology sensu De Pinna (1991)" — tested against phylogenetic congruence using a tree with turtles as archosauromorphs sister to Crocodylia + Aves. Tables 1–3 list every muscle in every taxon in the same row when interpreted as homologous, with bracketed alternative names from prior authors.

**Findings.**
1. The tetrapod pectoral/forelimb "body plan" was fixed very early. From urodeles to crocodilians, the overall muscle configuration is recognizable across 400 million years. Birds are the only conspicuous deviation.
2. Six axial-derived muscles connect axial skeleton to pectoral girdle across amphibians and reptiles: serratus anterior, "rhomboideus" (varies in presence), levator scapulae, opercularis, sternocoracoideus, costocoracoideus.
3. Plesiomorphic tetrapod forearm muscles insert on the radius, ulna, or proximal carpals. Mammals independently moved many insertions distally onto the metacarpals — and so did *Phyllomedusa* anurans, apparently correlated with fine digital control (prey manipulation, tree-frog grasping).
4. Human hands are less muscled than chimpanzee hands, lizard hands, and urodele hands. What distinguishes humans is the extensor pollicis brevis and flexor pollicis longus — forearm muscles with distal digit insertions, not a proliferation of intrinsic hand muscles.
5. Muscle attachment in the forelimb is often determined by **position** of the digit, not its embryological **identity**. The "abductor pollicis longus" in anurans attaches to digit 2 because digit 1 is absent; the "abductor digiti minimi" in urodeles attaches to digit 4 because digit 5 is absent. The muscle finds the most radial or most ulnar digit available.
6. Exception: chickens. The most ulnar digit lacks an abductor digiti minimi even though it is the most ulnar digit — digit 3/4 identity seems to matter here. The authors flag this as an open problem.
7. The head is the opposite case. Rhombomeric neural crest populations determine both muscle and insertion skeleton, so cranial muscle attachment is identity-bound. The forelimb is freer.
8. Bird pectoral/forelimb muscles depart most strongly from the plesiomorphic tetrapod pattern — the flight apparatus is the main reshaping event, not a general "reptile→amniote" divergence.

**Key figures / tables.**
- Fig. 1: phylogenetic framework, with turtles placed as archosauromorphs.
- Figs. 2–9: representative photographs and schematics of dissected specimens across all six groups (urodele, anuran, turtle, *Timon*, *Caiman*, *Gallus*).
- **Tables 1–3**: the operational output. Every row is one posited homologous muscle; every column a representative taxon. Bracketed synonyms from each prior author. This is the reference table for cross-reading the older literature.

**Limitations.** Some homology claims (esp. rhomboideus and palmaris longus across amphibians/reptiles) remain under-supported by developmental and innervation data. Bird digit-identity question is left open. Sampling, though extensive within lepidosaurs, is thin for birds (~6 species) and crocodilians (1 genus).

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Four uses:
1. **Tables as Rosetta stone.** Tables 1–3 translate muscle names across Gaupp, Howell, Walthall & Ashley-Ross, Meers, and Kardong conventions. Assign them alongside any dissection manual to stop students from reading the same muscle as six different things.
2. **Position vs. identity in limb patterning.** The anuran digit-2 "pollex" and urodele digit-4 "minimi" are excellent teaching examples of how muscle attachments track topology rather than deep identity. Ties cleanly to any digit-identity lecture (hox patterning, theropod-bird transition).
3. **Bird exceptionalism.** The observation that only birds show a major deviation from the conservative reptilian pattern reframes bird forelimb evolution as a single big functional reshaping rather than many incremental changes. Use when teaching flight origins.
4. **Humans are forelimb-conservative.** The data undercut any student intuition that human hands are "more complex." Primate/human novelty sits in the forearm (digital-insertion forearm muscles), not in the hand itself.

Best teaching figures: Tables 1–3 as a handout. Figs. 2–9 work as a paired visual with the tables.

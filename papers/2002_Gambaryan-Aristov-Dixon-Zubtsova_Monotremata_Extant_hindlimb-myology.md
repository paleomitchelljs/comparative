# Gambaryan et al. (2002) — Hind limb musculature in monotremes

## Citation

Gambaryan PP, Aristov AA, Dixon JM, Zubtsova GY. 2002. Peculiarities of the hind
limb musculature in monotremes: an anatomical description and functional approach.
*Russian Journal of Theriology* 1(1): 1–36.

## Question

How is the monotreme hind limb built, and why is it built that way? The paper is
half description and half mechanics, and the mechanics drive the description: the
authors dissected the muscles, put an articulated echidna hind limb through the two
extreme positions of the propulsive phase taken from published X-ray films (Jenkins
1970, Pridmore 1985), measured each muscle's length change and lever arm in both,
and filmed a platypus swimming.

## Taxa and material

**Two species, four specimens**, all in the Zoological Institute in St Petersburg:
*Tachyglossus aculeatus* (ZIN 85486, 85487) and *Ornithorhynchus anatinus*
(ZIN 85488, 85489). **Every muscle is described separately for each animal**, which
is what makes this source worth its length — it is not a monotreme composite but
two dissections set side by side, and where the genera differ the paper says so.

Nomenclature follows Schaller (1992), the veterinary standard, with a warning at the
outset that matters for every row scored here: **the cranial surface of the
monotreme femur faces dorsally and the caudal surface ventrally.** A reader who
takes "cranial surface of the femur" in its therian sense will put the muscle on the
wrong side of the bone.

## The central functional claim

**Monotreme terrestrial locomotion is driven by pronation of the femur, not by
retracting it.** Femoral retraction reaches more than 70° in sphenodontians,
crocodiles and squamates and 50–70° in therians; in monotremes it is **no more than
15°**, and what replaces it is rotation of the femur about its long axis. The
authors reach this from the lever-arm table rather than by assertion: the gluteus
superficialis, gluteus medius and pectineus all *shorten* during retraction instead
of lengthening, so retraction cannot be what they do.

Two consequences show up in the skeleton. The echidna's femur is flattened and its
head displaced medially, putting the rotation axis near the bone's medial edge and
lengthening every lever arm of a muscle inserting on the lateral edge. Its medial
epicondyle is elongated medially, lengthening the adductors' arms in turn. **The
iliopsoas is the only antagonist** of the whole gluteal and postfemoral mass during
propulsion.

The platypus, on land, moves in a pattern optimal for swimming rather than for
walking — which is the paper's opening line and its neatest result.

## The tuber calcanei argument

**Enlargement of the calcaneal tuber is usually read as an increase in the role of
ankle flexion. Gambaryan et al. argue it began as something else.** Their proposal
is that it enlarged first to resist *abduction* at the ankle — holding the hind foot
from slipping sideways under the symmetrical-diagonal gaits of an animal with widely
extended limbs — and that the flexion role came later, as mammalian limbs moved
towards the parasagittal. Enlargement in mammalian ancestors, and in crocodiles by
analogy, increased the load on the lateral toes; they note an undoubted connection
between the tuber calcanei and the fifth metatarsal in monotremes and in many
advanced cynodonts.

They also argue the process is **inverted relative to Theria**: the quadratus
plantae arises from the dorsal surface here and the plantar surface in therians, so
the monotreme dorsal surface may answer to the therian plantar one.

## What it gave this dataset

**The monotreme hind limb column, which did not exist.** Monotremata was the only
fully scored extant column in the corpus and it was scored forelimb only, from
Gambaryan et al. (2015) and Fahn-Lai et al. (2020). This closes the other half.

Occurrence rows on both *Tachyglossus aculeatus* and *Ornithorhynchus anatinus*
across the gluteal, lumbar, adductor, postfemoral, quadriceps, crural and pedal
groups — presence, division, attachments and, where the lever-arm analysis supports
it, action.

**Four skeletal elements**, all of them attachment sites this limb cannot be
described without:

- `third-trochanter` — takes the gluteus superficialis, the distal part of the
  gluteus medius and the caudofemoralis. Its **proximal and distal rugosities
  differ between the genera in step with the muscles on them**: the platypus has the
  better proximal rugosity and the echidna the better distal one, matching where
  each animal's gluteus medius ends. An osteological correlate that records which of
  two insertions dominated.
- `parafibular-process` — the busiest site in the limb, giving origin to eight
  muscles. Named by comparison with the marsupial and multituberculate parafibula
  without asserting the identity.
- `calcaneal-tuber` — see above.
- `psoas-minor-tubercle` — takes the psoas minor insertion and the gracilis anterior
  and pectineus origins; in the platypus it runs to more than half the length of the
  ilium.

**Two corrections to existing element records.** `trochanteric-fossa` is now scored
absent in Monotremata — in therians it takes the gemelli and the obturators, and it
is the landmark a palaeontologist would reach for to find them, so its absence here
means the correlate is not merely reduced but unavailable. And `patella` gained
Monotremata: it had been inheriting the record's `no` default purely because nothing
in the corpus described a monotreme knee, and the validator flagged the
contradiction the moment the muscle rows were entered.

## Homology arguments worth carrying

**The piriformis is absent in both monotremes**, and Coues (1870) described the
gluteus superficialis under that name. So these animals keep a caudofemoralis proper
and have not acquired the therian muscle usually read as its derivative — and in
them it lands on a third trochanter, where archosaurs put it on the fourth.

**The iliopsoas cannot be divided into psoas major and iliacus.** In therians the
two heads are usually separable proximally; here there is no separation at any
level, and in the platypus the muscle's insertion takes the whole cranial surface of
the trochanter minor, displacing the vastus medialis origin outright. The authors
call that insertion unique among mammals.

**Contrahentes, not adductores, for monotremes.** Their case: the name should mean
the group inserting on the *medial* sides of all the toes, which in a sprawling
animal keeps the foot from turning laterally during support. As limbs become
parasagittal that force disappears and the problem becomes keeping the toes
together; the contrahens of digit III drops out and muscles appear on the *lateral*
sides of digits I and II. Those serving digits IV and V they call homologous with
the reptilian and monotreme contrahentes without reservation; those on the lateral
sides of I and II they derive from the **interossei** instead, using the platypus's
own elaborate interossei dorsales as the model of that transformation. Against
Ribbing (1938), Jouffroy & Lessertisseur (1959) and Kyou-Jouffroy (1971), who
homologised the therian adductors with the reptilian contrahentes entire.

**They decline to homologise the long digital extensors at all.** Whether the
extensor digitorum superficialis or the profundus answers to the therian extensor
digitorum longus is left open in terms. What they will say is that the echidna's
four ankle transverse ligaments and the platypus's three carry the same tendons in
the same relations, so the two monotremes' muscles correspond to *each other* — an
argument from the retinacula rather than from the muscles. They reject Haines's
(1939) equation of the extensor digitorum superficialis with the peronei as contrary
to its topography.

**The adductor brevis of the platypus is offered as a model ancestor.** Move its
origin from the cranial edge of the obturator foramen onto the ischial tuberosity
and you have the marsupial, insectivore and rodent pattern; keep only the cranial
part of the origin and shift the proximal adductor magnus insertion lateral to the
brevis and you have the human one, which they attribute to erect posture on the
grounds that other primates do it differently.

## Where it disagrees with Cunningham (1882)

Both authors dissected these two species, so **this is not a case recency settles** —
an attachment is an observation and does not age. Three disagreements are recorded
on the rows rather than resolved:

| | Cunningham (1882) | Gambaryan et al. (2002) |
|---|---|---|
| Abductors of the little toe, *Tachyglossus* | absent, both of them, with no trace of fusion | two present, both from the tuber calcanei |
| Abductors of the little toe, *Ornithorhynchus* | one, from the calcaneal tuberosity | two, both from the semitendinosus tendon |
| Contrahentes/adductors, *Ornithorhynchus* | four, reaching phalanges | three, digit II's absent |

The `abductor-digiti-minimi-pes` row for the echidna is scored `uncertain` in
consequence. What can be said about the difference is that the two accounts looked
in different places: Cunningham enumerates the layers of the sole, and Gambaryan et
al. describe a group defined by its connection to the tuber calcanei — the region
Cunningham treats least.

## Limitations

**Table 1's forces are not usable.** It gives relative muscle weight — each muscle
as a percentage of total hind limb muscle mass — and a "calculated force (b) (in
dyn: 1 dyn = 10⁵ newton)", which is a mangled 10⁻⁵ and would make the largest figure
in the table well under a hundredth of a newton. The relative weights are
unambiguous and are quoted in the rows; **the forces are not entered anywhere**,
because no reading of the unit makes them plausible. Anyone wanting architecture for
this animal should go to Fahn-Lai et al. (2020) for the shoulder and take the
hind limb as unmeasured.

**No innervation.** The paper is topography and mechanics; apart from the popliteal
branch separating the peroneotibialis from the popliteus, nerves are not traced.
Cunningham (1882) remains the only nerve source for the monotreme foot.

**No obturator internus.** The `ischiotrochantericus` record is untouched by this
source. They mention the therian "m. obturatores" in passing when noting the absent
trochanteric fossa, but never describe an obturator internus in either animal, so
nothing is scored — absence of mention is not absence.

**Two specimens per species, no variation sampling** beyond one striking
observation: in one echidna the left semimembranosus posterior weighed twenty times
less than the right, which they read as a muscle capable of reducing to nothing.

**Extraction.** This is a two-column paper and `pdftotext -layout` interleaves the
columns line by line at page boundaries, producing coherent-looking hybrid
sentences — one of them attributed the caudofemoralis's origin and insertion to the
adductor magnus. The extraction in `papers/extracted/` is built column by column
with `pdftotext -x/-W` crops per page. **Do not re-run plain `-layout` on this
file.**

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The paper to reach for when a student asks what a mammal looked like before mammals
walked the way mammals walk. Every muscle has a therian name and almost none has the
therian arrangement: the popliteus arises from the fibula, the tibialis caudalis
arises entirely from the fibula, the obturator externus ends on the femoral shaft in
one genus and the lesser trochanter in the other, the caudofemoralis is still there
and the piriformis is not, and there is a muscle where the interosseous membrane
ought to be — in the echidna only, so that even Monotremata cannot be scored from
one animal.

Pair with **Gambaryan et al. (2015)** for the forelimb of the same animals by the
same lead author, with **Cunningham (1882)** for the marsupials and for the nerves
this paper does not trace, and with **Diogo & Molnar (2014)** for the tetrapod
hindlimb homology scheme the records themselves follow.

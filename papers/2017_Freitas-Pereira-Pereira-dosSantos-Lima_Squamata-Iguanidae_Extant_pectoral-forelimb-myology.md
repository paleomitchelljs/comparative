# Freitas et al. (2017) — Pectoral girdle and forelimb muscles of *Iguana iguana*

## Citation

Freitas LM, Pereira DKS, Pereira KF, Santos OP, Lima FC. 2017. Muscular anatomy of
the pectoral girdle and forelimb of *Iguana i. iguana* (Squamata: Iguanidae).
*Bioscience Journal*.

## Why it was reached for

**The only source here that dissects *Iguana* specifically.** The lepidosaur column
is otherwise built on varanids, on Osawa's tuatara, and on Abdala & Diogo's survey,
whose lepidosaur exemplar is *Timon lepidus*. An iguanid dissection is a different
animal, and under species-level scoring it gets its own rows rather than displacing
anyone.

## Taxa and material

**Two post-mortem specimens of *Iguana iguana***, donated from a scientific breeding
facility and dissected — skin reflected, fasciae removed, muscles individualised.

Muscles identified: pectoralis, deltoideus clavicularis, deltoideus scapularis,
trapezius, latissimus dorsi, coracobrachialis brevis, coracobrachialis longus,
serratus thoracis, levator scapulae, biceps brachii and triceps brachii.

## Scored

1 occurrence row on *Iguana iguana* — `pectoralis`, with its two portions: the
superficial from the entire midventral aspect of the posterior process of the
interclavicle and the posterior border of its lateral process, the deep from the
posteriormost mesosternum.

**One row from a paper describing eleven muscles is a thin yield, and the reason is
worth recording**: most of what it describes was already scored for *Iguana* from
Russell & Bauer (2008), which covers the same animal in the same region at higher
resolution. This source confirms and supplies a second observer rather than opening
new ground. The remaining muscles are available if a second independent scoring of
the iguanid forelimb is ever wanted.

## Limitations

- **Two specimens, one locality.** Nothing here on variation.
- **Written as a descriptive anatomy paper**, not a comparative one, so it makes no
  homology claims and carries no `homologyScope`.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The green iguana is a common teaching animal and this is the paper that describes
its shoulder as such. Pair with **Russell & Bauer (2008)** for the same region
across normal-limbed squamates and the tuatara, and with **Zaaf et al. (1999)** for
what varies between two geckos.

# Bodenham et al. (2026) — *Galahadosuchus jonesi*, a second saltoposuchid from the Bristol fissures

## Citation

Bodenham EH, Spiekman SNF, Maidment SCR, Upchurch P, Mannion PD. 2026. A second
species of non-crocodyliform crocodylomorph from the Late Triassic fissure deposits
of southwestern UK. *The Anatomical Record*.

## Why it was reached for

**It is the first source in this dataset for the stem-crocodylomorph column.** The
Late Triassic–Early Jurassic fissures of the Bristol Channel area yield
predominantly small-bodied forms that matter for the early evolution of several
tetrapod clades, and nothing else here reaches that node.

## Taxa and material

*Galahadosuchus jonesi*, **NHMUK PV R 10002**, a saltoposuchid from Cromhall
Quarry. Compared directly with *Dromicosuchus* (NCSM 13733) and *Terrestrisuchus*.

## What kind of source it is

**Osteological rather than myological** — 10 origin/insertion mentions in the whole
paper. What makes it scorable is that it identifies the **muscle-bearing correlates
on the coracoid and femur** and compares them against the two other saltoposuchids,
so the correlate is anchored in more than one animal.

## Scored

3 occurrence rows on *Galahadosuchus* sp., all `inferred`:

- **`caudofemoralis`** — from a low, proximodistally elongate fourth trochanter
  about a quarter of the way down the posteromedial femur, bearing a shallow medial
  pit. A Level I inference. **Its modest development relative to the crocodylian
  condition is the observation, not its presence**, which was never in doubt.
- **`biceps-brachii`** and **`coracobrachialis`** — from the coracoid correlates.

Three rows from one paper is the correct yield for an osteological description, and
the column exists now where it did not before.

## Limitations

- **One specimen, and a fossil.** Every row is a reconstruction.
- **A correlate's absence is not a muscle's absence** — see `METHODS.md`. Nothing
  here licenses scoring anything `no`.
- **Dating of the fissure deposits remains contentious**, which the authors say
  plainly. That affects where the taxon sits in time, not what its bones show.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Useful for showing that a single well-described bone can support a defensible
muscle statement, and for how narrow that statement has to be: a trochanter's size
tells you about the *development* of the muscle that pulled on it, not about
whether it was there. Pair with **Godoy et al. (2016)** on a crocodyliform and
**Liparini & Schultz (2013)** on a stem pseudosuchian.

# Ghetie et al. (1976) — Anatomical atlas of domestic birds

## Citation

Ghetie V, Chitescu St, Cotofan V, Hillebrand A. 1976. *Atlas de anatomie a
pasarilor domestice / Atlas d'anatomie des oiseaux domestiques / Anatomical atlas
of domestic birds*. Editura Academiei RSR, Bucharest. Four languages: Romanian,
French, English, Russian.

## What it is, and what it is not

A **plate atlas** of the domestic fowl, duck and goose. Osteology, arthrology,
myology and the rest presented as labelled figures.

**It carries no descriptive myology.** A density check over the full 154-page
extraction returns essentially zero origin or insertion statements: the anatomy is
in the plates, not in prose. Nothing can be scored from text that does not exist.

This is worth recording as a finding rather than as a disappointment. The atlas was
the obvious remedy for the *Gallus* column — it is in `papers/`, it is about
chickens, and it was cited here five times. Opening it is what showed that the
avian rows citing Abdala & Diogo's synonymy alone cannot be closed from anything
the corpus currently holds.

## Scored

5 occurrence rows on *Gallus domesticus* — pectoralis, supracoracoideus, deltoideus
scapularis, latissimus dorsi, coracobrachialis — carrying names and presence. The
attachments on those rows come from elsewhere; this source supplies the vocabulary
and the confirmation that the muscle is there.

## The consequence for the Aves column

**The *Gallus* rows are source-limited in the strict sense.** Nothing else in the
corpus describes a chicken. The way to grow the avian column is therefore the
species that *are* described — the swan (Matsuoka & Hasegawa 2007), the loon
(McKitrick 1991), the penguin (Schreiweis 1982), the crane (Fisher & Goodman 1955),
the tinamou (Widrig et al. 2023), the pigeon (Jones et al. 2019) — which is what
the recent passes did.

`WORKLIST.md` lists this among the sources that are in the corpus and are not row
sources, so that nobody plans a pass around it again.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Genuinely useful in a teaching context, which is worth separating from its
uselessness here: labelled plates in four languages are a good way to learn avian
topography. It is a reference for what a structure *looks* like, not for where a
muscle attaches.

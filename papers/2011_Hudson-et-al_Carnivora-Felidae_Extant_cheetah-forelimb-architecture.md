# Hudson et al. (2011) — Functional anatomy of the cheetah forelimb

## Citation

Hudson PE, Corr SA, Payne-Davis RC, Clancy SN, Lane E, Wilson AM. 2011. Functional
anatomy of the cheetah (*Acinonyx jubatus*) forelimb. *Journal of Anatomy* 218:
375–385. Companion to the hindlimb paper in the same issue (218: 363–374).

## Question

The cheetah reaches 29 m s⁻¹; the racing greyhound, an animal of similar mass, only
17. Where in the musculoskeletal anatomy is the difference?

## Taxa and material

Cheetah and greyhound forelimbs. Muscle mass, fascicle length and moment arms
measured, giving muscle volume, PCSA, and estimates of joint torque and rotational
velocity. Bone lengths, masses and mid-shaft cross-sectional areas as well.

## Findings worth carrying

**The proximal limb is built to resist force, not to generate speed.** It contains
many large-PCSA muscles on long moment arms, which suits resisting the large ground
reaction forces at the joints rather than swinging the limb quickly.

**The long-fibred serratus ventralis** is the species difference they dwell on.
They suggest it translates the scapula along the rib cage, as has been observed in
domestic cats, effectively lengthening the limb — a mechanism that adds stride
length without adding bone.

## Scored

This paper supplied **the first architecture data in the dataset**: 4 records
carrying mass, fascicle length, pennation and PCSA.

**The rows are not on the cheetah.** They sit on *Galictis cuja* and *Rattus
norvegicus*, the animals whose attachments the same records carry, with the
architecture attached where the source measured it. Read the `architecture` block
as the cheetah's and the attachments as the named species'.

## Limitations

- **Two species, one comparison.** Everything here is cheetah-versus-greyhound;
  neither is a general carnivoran.
- **Architecture, not attachment.** Only 11 origin/insertion mentions in the whole
  paper. It is not a row source and `WORKLIST.md` says so.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Good for the point that the fastest animal is not the one with the biggest muscles
in the obvious places. Pair with the hindlimb companion, where the surprise is
sharper still.

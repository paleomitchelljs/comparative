# Schreiweis (1982) — Appendicular musculature of penguins

## Citation

Schreiweis DO. 1982. A comparative study of the appendicular musculature of
penguins (Aves: Sphenisciformes). *Smithsonian Contributions to Zoology* 341,
46 pp., 19 figures.

## Question

What is the gross anatomy of every appendicular muscle across the Sphenisciformes,
and what do the variations say about relationships within the order?

## Taxa and material

**14 species, all six living genera.** ***Eudyptes pachyrhynchus*** is the type
for which all appendicular muscles are described and most are illustrated; other
taxa are given as differences from that condition.

Similarities and differences are scored quantitatively over **47 items in the wing
and 41 in the leg**, following Hudson et al.'s (1966) method, and reported as
cumulative difference scores and correlation coefficients.

## Findings worth carrying

**Wing and leg give the same answer.** The cumulative scores and correlation
coefficients from the two limbs run closely parallel, which is a real result: the
two appendages are not independent evidence of the same phylogeny, but their
agreement means neither is being driven by a locomotor specialisation the other
lacks.

**Sphenisciformes are a uniform group**, and the comparison supports the existing
classification of 14 species in 6 genera.

## Scored

8 occurrence rows, mostly on *Eudyptes pachyrhynchus* — serratus anterior,
pectoralis, supracoracoideus, deltoideus scapularis, scapulohumeralis posterior.
The rows are detailed enough to name individual fascicles and the ribs they come
from: the serratus profundus is four flat fleshy fascicles from the last two
cervical and first two thoracic ribs, dorsal to the uncinate processes.

**This source was once refused for being too derived to stand for Aves.** Under
the old model, where a clade had one row, a penguin could not be scored without
displacing the chicken. Species-level scoring removed that objection, and the
penguin is now one avian column among several. That was a judgement a dataset
should not have been making, and roughly 30 further rows in this paper are still
available on the same reasoning.

## Limitations

- **The wing is the most derived in the collection.** A penguin flipper is not a
  general bird wing, and its rows should be read as one animal's, which is what
  species-level scoring now makes them.
- **Cumulative difference scores are a phenetic method.** The tentative phylogeny
  offered is of its time; the descriptive anatomy is what this dataset takes.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The best available contrast to a flying wing: pair it with **Matsuoka & Hasegawa
(2007)** on the whooper swan and the same records read very differently, with the
supracoracoideus tendon through the triosseal canal doing a wholly different job in
each. Also a good illustration of why "the bird condition" is a phrase to distrust.

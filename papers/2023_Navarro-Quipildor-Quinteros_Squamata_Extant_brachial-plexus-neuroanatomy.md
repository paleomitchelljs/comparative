# Navarro, Quipildor & Quinteros 2023 — Neuroanatomy of the brachial plexus in South American lizards

## Citation
Navarro EA, Quipildor M, Quinteros S. 2023. Description of the neuroanatomy of the brachial plexus in South American lizards. Phylogenetic implications. *Journal of Morphology* 284: e21629. doi:10.1002/jmor.21629.

## Question
Studies of the vertebrate nervous system in ectothermic sauropsids are rare, and the brachial plexus anatomy in particular has been sampled sparsely and inconsistently across squamate families (Table 1 in the paper shows previous work scored plexus branches as 2–6 depending on the family and author). Can detailed dissection of the brachial plexus in closely related South American lizard clades recover phylogenetic signal useful for clade diagnosis?

> "Few studies considered the anatomy of the nerve plexuses and musculature associated with them in ectothermic sauropsids. … Based on this, potentially useful characters for phylogenetic analysis were described. Our results show that the brachial plexus can be formed by four, five, or six nerve branches."

> "Phylogenetic analyzes revealed 31 potential synapomorphies. There exists evidence that neuroanatomy studies in a phylogenetic context could provide useful information helping to elucidate the relationships between taxonomic groups."

## Taxa and material
68 specimens across 3 closely related families (the Iguania clade Liolaemoidea sensu lato):
- **Tropiduridae**: *Tropidurus melanopleurus*.
- **Leiosauridae**: *Diplolaemus cf. sexcintus*, *D. leopardinus*.
- **Liolaemidae — Liolaemus** (subgenus *Liolaemus sensu stricto*): *L. cf. kriegi*, *L. umbrifer*, *L. yanalcu*; (subgenus *Eulaemus*): *L. kingii* (*L. lineomaculatus* section), *L. multicolor*, *L. cf. scrocchii*, *L. vulcanus* (*L. montanus* section), *L. albiceps*, *L. irregularis* (*L. boulengeri* series).
- **Liolaemidae — Phymaturus**: *P. felixi*, *P. sitesi* (*P. patagonicus* group), *P. denotatus*, *P. laurenti* (*P. palluma* group).

16 terminal taxa, specimens from the Museo de Ciencias Naturales de la Universidad Nacional de Salta (MCN) and the Instituto de Bio y Geo Ciencias del NOA (IBIGEO), Argentina.

## Time frame
Extant.

## Methods
- **Differential Sudan Black B staining** — a technique that stains nerves black while leaving muscles transparent and marking bones/cartilages differently. Allows fine nerve-branch identification that pure dissection would miss.
- **Conventional dissection** under stereomicroscope for larger specimens where clearing + Sudan Black staining was impractical.
- **Stereomicroscope photography** (Olympus DP25), digital caliper measurements (0.02 mm).
- **Nerve nomenclature** following Quipildor et al. 2020 — each thoracic spinal nerve is numbered and its branches coded (e.g., T4.2 = branch 2 of thoracic nerve 4; T7–T8.1 = union of T7 and branch 1 of T8).
- **Vertebral numbering** per Hoffstetter & Gasc 1969, Arantes 2016.
- **Muscle identification** following Hoyos 1990, Renous-Lécuru 1972, Baumel 1993, Arantes 2016, Abdala & Moro 2006, Diogo & Abdala 2007, Abdala et al. 2010, Freitas et al. 2017.
- **Phylogenetic analysis** in TNT (Tree Analysis using New Technology; Goloboff & Catalano 2016) under parsimony with implicit enumeration, equal weights, 70 discrete characters × 16 terminal taxa, characters discriminated into polymorphic binary (PB), non-polymorphic binary (NPB), and polymorphic multistate (PM). Character optimization on a composite metatree derived from Conrad 2008, Gauthier 2012, Pyron 2013, Simões & Pyron 2021, Zheng & Wiens 2016, Abdala 2007–2020, Lobo 2001–2010, Olave 2014, Portelli & Quinteros 2018, Quinteros 2013–2020.

## Findings (numbered)

1. **The brachial plexus in these lizards is formed by 4, 5, or 6 nerve branches**, originating from thoracic vertebrae T3–T9 depending on species and individual (Table 2). Two primary nervous trunks — **anterior** (from T4–T5 in most species, sometimes T3–T5) and **posterior** (from T6–T8 or T6–T9) — are universal in the sample, joined by a communicating branch that arises from the anterior primary trunk.

2. **Main nerves identified**: from the **anterior primary trunk** → *supracoracoid* (nscor), *subscapulocoracoid* (nssc) → innervate pectoral girdle + shoulder. From the **posterior primary trunk** → *circumflex* (nc), *radial* (nr), *brachial flexor trunk* (bft) → subsequent branches *interosseous* (ni), *median* (nm), *ulnar* (nu). Total nerves named: *brachial flexor trunk*, *circumflex*, *interosseous*, *median*, *radial*, *subscapulocoracoid*, *supracoracoid*, *ulnar* plus the parent primary trunks.

3. **Muscles innervated by these nerves (identified across the sample)**: biceps brachii, deltoideus scapularis, interosseous, latissimus dorsi, levator scapulae, pectoralis, serratus thoracis, trapezius, triceps longus caudalis, triceps longus lateralis, extensor carpi ulnaris, flexor digitorum longo, extensor digitorum longo, extensor carpi radialis, short digital extensors I–V. The paper gives per-nerve innervation scores per species (Tables 3–4 of the full paper, not in our extracted pages).

4. **The supracoracoid nerve entry pattern is a Liolaemidae synapomorphy.** In Liolaemidae species, the supracoracoid nerve enters the coracoid foramen, then divides into two fine branches that penetrate the pectoral fenestra and emerge posteriorly on the **external face** of the pectoral girdle — where they run along the lower portion of the levator scapulae muscle innervating it. This pattern is shared by all liolaemids sampled and constitutes a potential synapomorphy for the family.

5. **31 potential synapomorphies recovered** across the 16-taxon × 70-character matrix, supporting various nodes in the metatree. The brachial plexus carries recoverable phylogenetic signal even among closely related lizards — at minimum for diagnosing Liolaemidae and some subgenus-level clades.

6. **Intra-specific variability exists.** Several species show 2–3 different brachial plexus formulas in different individuals (e.g., *T. melanopleurus*: T4-T5-T6-T7-T8 or T4-T5-T6-T7-T8-T9; *D. leopardinus*: T3-T4-T5-T6-T7 or T4-T5-T6-T7 or T4-T5-T6-T7-T8). Some of this variation is scored as polymorphic multistate characters in the TNT matrix. This undermines simple "one nerve count per species" characters and reinforces the importance of sample size.

## Limitations
- **16 terminal taxa**, all within a single Iguania subclade (Liolaemoidea). Generalization to other squamate families requires the comparative work this paper sets as a template, not its own data.
- **Sudan Black differential staining** works best on small specimens; larger *Tropidurus* were dissected conventionally, introducing methodological heterogeneity between taxa.
- Intra-specific polymorphism coding is conservative; polymorphic multistate characters reduce the decisiveness of phylogenetic signal.
- Character definitions of plexus anatomy (branch counts, union patterns, root origins) are inherently somewhat arbitrary — different workers might code the same nerve complex differently.
- No developmental or molecular data. The phylogenetic signal is purely from adult gross anatomy.
- The supporting information (S1–S3) with specimen list + abbreviations list + character list was not in the extracted pages; full list of 70 characters not reproduced here.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching
Two teaching uses:

1. **Nerve plexus anatomy carries phylogenetic signal.** Traditional phylogenetic characters in squamates are osteological + molecular; nervous-system characters are much less commonly used. This paper demonstrates that the brachial plexus can diagnose family- and subgenus-level clades. Pair with 2017_Arantes-et-al (iguanid brachial plexus) and 1993_Wake (caecilian cranial-spinal nerves) for the "nervous-system characters in comparative anatomy" mini-module.

2. **Methodology: Sudan Black B staining for nerves.** This is the one technique in the paper that should be added to the undergraduate dissection toolbox. Pair with 1995_SongParenti (triple stain nerve protocol) for a nerve-visualization methods unit.

Best used as reference + slide source. Figure 1 (brachial plexus schematic of *L. yanalcu*) is the teaching asset worth keeping on the slide deck for the squamate limb module. Pair with 2004_Al-Hassawi (squamate craniocervical osteology + myology), 2017_Freitas-Pereira et al. (iguanid pectoral myology), 2021_Dutel et al. (squamate cranial FEA), and 2023_Kepa et al. (Komodo dragon forelimb) for a complete squamate limb / plexus / locomotion lecture.

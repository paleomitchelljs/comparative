# Schilling (2011) — Evolution of the axial system in craniates

## Citation

Schilling N. 2011. Evolution of the axial system in craniates: morphology and
function of the perivertebral musculature. *Frontiers in Zoology* 8: 4.
doi:10.1186/1742-9994-8-4. Open access.

## Question

The axial musculoskeletal system is the plesiomorphic locomotor engine of the
vertebrate body. What happened to it across two transformations — the increasing
regionalisation of the axial skeleton, and the shift from axial-based to
appendage-based locomotion — and what did those do to muscle *function* rather
than just to gross anatomy?

## What kind of source it is

**A review that maps character states onto a phylogeny.** It integrates anatomical
and physiological data — muscle fibre types, activation patterns — with
gross-anatomical and kinematic findings for pivotal craniate taxa, and infers the
character set of each last common ancestor. It is not a dissection report, and
that distinction has already cost this dataset once (below).

`homologyScope: true`: it reconciles axial muscle organisation across craniates,
which is more than one taxon and is its stated purpose.

## Findings worth carrying

**Mobilisation is the plesiomorphic function and is retained in every craniate.**
What gets *added* is stabilisation — of the trunk against inertial and extrinsic
limb-muscle forces and against gravity once animals leave water, and in mammals
against sagittal mobility and a parasagittal limb posture.

**Complexity increases in two measurable ways rather than one.** Fibre angulation
becomes increasingly oblique, and fatigue-resistant fibres segregate into the
deeper regions of the muscle. The loss of superficial fatigue-resistant fibres may
be tied to the gross reorganisation of the axial musculature across the tetrapod
transition.

That is a useful counter to the dataset's usual attachment-first framing: here the
evolutionary signal is in fibre type and activation, neither of which has a field
in this schema.

## Scored, and the correction that matters

14 occurrence rows across **ten species**, all axial: the epaxial and hypaxial
fields, the caudal musculature, the transversospinalis and the longissimus.

**This source is the reason the base-layer rule exists in its current form.** It
had been carrying crocodylian, avian, actinopterygian and cheetah rows that came
from **his Figure 1 — a cladogram synthesis with no animal behind it**. Those rows
were removed. Seven stayed, re-attributed to the animals his Figures 2 and 3
actually show: *Myxine glutinosa*, *Etmopterus spinax*, *Ambystoma tigrinum*,
*Microtus arvalis* and *Canis familiaris*.

The lesson generalises and is in `MINING.md`: **a citation records where a claim
was read, not where it was observed.** A review that states the condition for a
clade is not a specimen, and scoring it as one turns a synthesis into a
dissection nobody performed.

## Limitations

- **Attachment data is not what this paper is for.** Most rows it supports carry
  presence and division but no attachment rows, correctly.
- **The crocodylian and avian axial columns are now empty**, and that is a source
  gap rather than a coverage regression — nothing in `papers/` describes
  crocodylian trunk musculature. Boumans et al. (2015) would restore the avian
  *neck*. See `WORKLIST.md`.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The best short statement available of why the axial system deserves attention in a
course that is otherwise about limbs: it is the engine the limbs were added to,
and its function changed more than its anatomy did. Pair with **Cieri (2018)** for
a scored varanid trunk and with **Omura et al. (2014)** for urodele trunk variety.

# Ercoli, Álvarez, Stefanini, Busker & Morales 2014 — Lesser grison forelimb myology

## Citation

Ercoli MD, Álvarez A, Stefanini MI, Busker F, Morales MM. 2014. Muscular anatomy of the forelimbs of the lesser grison (*Galictis cuja*), and a functional and phylogenetic overview of Mustelidae and other Caniformia. *Journal of Mammalian Evolution*. DOI 10.1007/s10914-014-9257-6.

## Question

Three nested questions. (a) What does the forelimb myology of *Galictis cuja* — a short-legged South American ictonychine mustelid that hunts by bound/half-bound locomotion and carries relatively large prey — actually look like, muscle by muscle? (b) Where do the weasel-like mustelid autapomorphies and the mustelid subfamily synapomorphies lie in the forelimb? (c) What functional signal can be read from the mass distribution — i.e., how does a half-bounder's forelimb muscle-mass budget differ from a cursorial, digging, climbing, or aquatic caniform's? Follows the authors' 2013 monograph on the hindlimb + lumbar + tail regions of the same species (cf. 2012_Ercoli-et-al note).

## Taxa and material

- **Primary**: *Galictis cuja* (lesser grison), three whole-body specimens from Argentinean collections — MACN-Ma 25483 (male, "MASTO 92"), MACN-Ma 28.163 (female), MACN-Ma 39.207. Specimens 1 and 2 weighed muscle-by-muscle after dissection (Supporting Information Table S1).
- **Comparative dissections (mustelids)**: *Aonyx cinerea*, *Enhydra lutris*, *Gulo gulo*, *Lutra lutra*, *Martes* spp., *Mellivora capensis*, *Mustela* spp., *Taxidea taxus*, *Vormela peregusna*.
- **Comparative dissections (non-mustelid caniforms)**: *Acinonyx jubatus*, *Canis familiaris*, *Leopardus pardalis*, *Ursus americanus*.
- **Literature corpus**: every prior mustelid forelimb dissection (Table 1) from Meckel 1828 through Moore et al. 2013 — 10 subfamily groupings across 14 species.

Nomenclature: Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria 2005; where NAV names conflict with homology (e.g., "adductor digiti secundi" for flexor digitorum brevis manus II) the authors use the homology-preserving name (Waibl et al. 2005; Spoor & Badoux 1986a; Fisher et al. 2009; Diogo & Abdala 2010).

## Time frame

Extant; extinct mustelid lineages considered via character optimization on the composite phylogeny of Flynn et al. 2005 + Koepfli et al. 2007 + Sato et al. 2012. Fourteen muscular characters mapped with TNT (Goloboff et al. 2008).

## Methods

1. **Gross dissection** of each forelimb muscle, per specimen, with intraspecific variation noted (Table S2). Muscle maps drawn onto thoracic + scapular + humeral + ulnar-radial + manus skeletal outlines (Figs. 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 11). Superficial → deep whole-forelimb illustrations (Figs. 3, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12).

2. **Dry mass weighing** per muscle (specimens 1 and 2); percentages relative to total intrinsic forelimb mass in Table 2. Muscle mass relative to total forelimb musculature plotted against locomotor category (cursorial / half-bounder / galloper / digger / climber / swimmer) for 13 carnivoran taxa in Fig. 13.

3. **Character optimization.** 14 myological characters (definitions in Fig. S1) mapped onto the composite mustelid phylogeny with TNT. Characters scored as ordered except four (a, e, g, n). Ambiguous reconstructions flagged with "?" on Fig. 14.

## Findings

1. **Muscle mass profile of *Galictis cuja*** (Table 2; Fig. 13). Dominated by extrinsic + protractor muscles, weak shoulder rotator cuff. Top single-muscle percentages of total intrinsic forelimb mass: latissimus dorsi 26.1%, pectoralis profundus caudal 18.3%, triceps brachii caput longum 13.9%, serratus ventralis cervicis 12.7%, cleidocephalicus pars cervicalis 12.5%, pectorales superficiales caudal 9.4%, triceps brachii caput laterale 8.6%, subscapularis 7.3%, suprapinatus principal 7.1%, trapezius pars thoracica 7.1%, cleidocephalicus pars mastoidea 6.5%, omotransversarius 6.1%, trapezius cervicalis 6.1%, rhomboideus cervicis 5.8%, infraspinatus 5.1%, triceps brachii caput angulare 4.9%. The "rotator cuff" (supraspinatus + infraspinatus + teres minor + teres major + subscapularis + coracobrachialis + articularis humeri) is weak. Extensor-vs-flexor balance at the elbow is tilted toward extension (long + lateral + angular + accessorius triceps heads).

2. **Mustelid forelimb synapomorphies identified or revised** (Fig. 14):
   - **Acquisition of the angular head of triceps brachii** is a *mustelid + mephitid* synapomorphy (not unique to mustelids as older accounts claimed), reflecting bounding + crouched-locomotion recruitment. Also present in some *Conepatus chinga*.
   - **Loss of articularis humeri** at the Mustelidae + Mephitidae node, with secondary re-acquisition in the Gulonines and also in *Galictis cuja* (first record within Ictonychinae, unique within weasel-like mustelids).
   - **Acquisition of coracobrachialis** at Mustelidae–Taxidea node (but ambiguous reconstruction).
   - **Reduction of origin of extensor digiti I et II** at Mustelidae–Taxidea node.
   - **Separation of rhomboideus capitis from rhomboideus cervicis** at Mustelidae minus Taxidiinae node (*T. taxus* retains the ancestral fused condition).
   - **Loss of flexor digitorum brevis manus** at Mustelidae–Taxidea (but see Ta. taxus discussion).
   - **Loss of tensor fasciae antebrachii** at the Mustelinae + Ictonychinae + Lutrinae node.
   - **Extension of origin of extensor digiti I et II** at the same node.
   - **Brachioradialis with proximal humeral origin extending onto the ectepicondylar crest** is a **derived-otter** synapomorphy (*Enhydra*, *Aonyx*, *Lutra lutra*), approached (but not reached) in *Lontra canadensis*. Supports Lutrinae's aquatic clade.
   - **Fusion of pronator teres and flexor carpi radialis origins** at the Gulonines + *Ailurus fulgens* + *Ailuropoda* node (convergent).

3. **Rhomboideus profundus is a Musteloidea synapomorphy** (ambiguous at higher node). Present in mustelids + *Potos flavus* + mephitids. Absent in *Ailurus fulgens*. The muscle adds a protraction-rotation moment at the shoulder; consistent with the symmetrical-gait precision-stride demand of weasel-like mustelids moving through galleries or searching epigean cover for prey.

4. **The "weasel-like" musculature profile** (ictonychines + mustelines). Strong extrinsic protractors (rhomboideus capitis, cervicis, profundus, trapezius cervicalis, cleidocephalicus); reduced extrinsic retractor musculature (rhomboideus thoracis vestigial in *Ga. cuja*, *Mustela vison*, *Mu. putorius* — thoracic portion of rhomboideus is <13% of total rhomboideus mass in *Ga. cuja*); weak intrinsic shoulder; relatively strong extensor elbow muscles with long + angular triceps heads. Pattern tracks the half-bound running gait: forelimbs absorb landing impact and extend the stride rather than propelling the body, which is driven by the powerful lumbar extensor apparatus described in Ercoli et al. 2012.

5. **Extrinsic musculature is more phylogenetically informative than intrinsic** in Mustelidae. Intrinsic forelimb muscles (flexor carpi, extensor carpi, pronators, supinators, intrinsic hand muscles) vary very little across caniforms in configuration — their functional signal comes from mass, not topology. Extrinsic muscles (trapezius, rhomboideus, pectorales, cleidocephalicus, serratus ventralis) vary in number of bellies, origin extent, insertion position, and fiber direction, and these variations map onto the phylogeny. Implication for anatomy teaching: when using muscle for phylogenetic inference, score the extrinsic set first.

6. **Specific nomenclatural corrections and identifications:**
   - *Galictis cuja* has **both abductor digiti II and abductor digiti IV** in the manus — first recorded for any weasel-like mustelid; probably more widespread in Musteloidea than the bibliography suggests.
   - The "flexor sublimis digitorum" of Cuvier & Laurillard 1849 is **not** a humeral origin of flexor digitorum superficialis — it is a humeral head of flexor digitorum profundus fused with the superficial flexor common tendon. No true humeral origin of flexor digitorum superficialis exists in Caniformia.
   - The "tensor fasciae antebrachii" of otters (Howard 1973) is not homologous with the muscle of the same name in canids and ursids — in ictonychines + lutrines it is lost, and what *looks* like tensor fasciae antebrachii in the caput angulare region is probably a migrated triceps brachii caput angulare.
   - *Ga. cuja* is the first record of **palmaris longus with both bellies confirmed** in a weasel-like mustelid.

7. **Functional-profile comparison** (Fig. 13). The relative mass of each muscle group plotted against carnivoran locomotor mode shows:
   - Cursorial caniforms (*Acinonyx*, *Canis*): highest shoulder rotator cuff, lowest forelimb extrinsic musculature relative to total.
   - Digging mustelids (*Taxidea*, *Mellivora*): extensors of elbow hypertrophied; strong brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis.
   - Climbers (*Ailurus fulgens*, gulonines, some procyonids): strong biceps + brachialis + brachioradialis; retained coracobrachialis.
   - Swimmers (*Enhydra*, *Lutra*, *Aonyx*): massive triceps brachii caput angulare; brachioradialis origin migrated proximally; pectorales superficiales inverted into "crossed type."
   - Half-bounders (*Ga. cuja*, *Mustela*, *Vormela*): reduced intrinsic shoulder, strong extrinsic protractors, reduced retractors; the *Ga. cuja* profile sits between terrestrial mustelines and semiaquatic lutrines in multiple axes.

## Limitations

- **n = 3 specimens**, only two weighed. Individual variation in muscle mass is substantial; e.g., rhomboideus cervicis 2.5% in specimen 1 vs. ~1% in specimen 2. Percentages in Table 2 are two-specimen averages; single-specimen claims would not survive the variation.
- **One specimen damaged** (MACN-Ma 39.207) — axial musculature could not be fully described; another specimen (MACN-Ma 28.163) had poor digital-musculature preservation.
- **Intraspecific vs. interspecific variation not systematically decoupled.** Table S2 flags individual differences, but without a larger series the authors cannot always say whether a reported feature of *Ga. cuja* is species-typical or specimen-typical.
- **Character optimization rests on the Sato et al. 2012 phylogeny**, which places Taxidiinae at the base of Mustelidae and supports the Ictonychinae + Lutrinae sister relationship. Under competing topologies (e.g., Bryant et al. 1993 cladistic morphology), the 14 optimized characters would re-score differently — several of the proposed synapomorphies are topology-dependent.
- **Absence claims depend on what the original describer called the muscle.** "*Ta. taxus* lacks flexor digitorum brevis manus" has been disputed; Hall 1927 may have described it under a different name. The authors flag this for at least five muscles.
- **Locomotor-mode–mass correlations in Fig. 13 are descriptive, not statistical.** No phylogenetic-independent-contrast analysis; the pattern is visually compelling but not tested against tree non-independence.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Useful as the definitive reference for *Galictis cuja* forelimb dissection and, with the 2012 Ercoli paper on hindlimb + tail + lumbar region, forms a complete single-species myology that can anchor an entire comparative-anatomy term.

Three specific teaching uses:

First, a **"soft-tissue phylogenetic characters" case study.** Fig. 14 shows the payoff of careful dissection: 14 muscle characters distributed across the Mustelidae tree, with at least a half-dozen synapomorphies identifiable at each major subfamily node. Pair with Diogo & Molnar 2014 (FL–HL serial homology deconstruction, 2014_Diogo-Molnar note) for the methodological counterpoint — topological correspondence first, but innervation + development layered on top before you trust a homology claim.

Second, a **locomotor-mode signal in muscle mass** lecture. Fig. 13 is the single best recent figure comparing cursorial / half-bounder / digger / climber / swimmer caniforms muscle-by-muscle. Makes explicit the "extrinsic protractor vs. rotator cuff" trade-off that differentiates weasel-like hunters from the cursorial stereotype.

Third, a **"why the forelimb of a weasel is not a small dog"** lecture. *Canis familiaris* dominates introductory comparative mammalian anatomy; *Galictis cuja* shows what a mammal built around the half-bound, large-prey-carrying, tunnel-following niche actually looks like. Strong extrinsic retractors, proportionally huge latissimus dorsi, triceps brachii with six well-separated bellies, brachioradialis proximally extended, an extra belly in palmaris longus, abductor digiti II and IV. Students should meet the non-cursorial caniform bauplan early; this paper + Ercoli et al. 2012 make that feasible.

Cross-reference the 2012 Ercoli paper's axial + hindlimb findings when teaching the forelimb signal — the two papers together give the full weasel-like mustelid postcranial picture.

## Quotes (selected, pre-summary extraction)

- Abstract: "As other ictonychines and mustelines, *Galictis cuja* possesses stronger and subdivided protractors and sagittal rotators of the forelimbs, as well as shoulder and elbow extensors. These features allow for resistance in landing during running bounds and increase the stride length during epigean and subterranean crouched locomotion."
- Abstract: "Weakness of some retractors and intrinsic flexors is related to a relatively minor importance of the forelimbs as propellers during bounding and the lack of other specializations. The configuration of the rhomboideus and the absence of coracobrachialis seem to be informative at the subfamiliar level within Mustelidae."
- Discussion: "Weasel and weasel-like mustelids present bound or half-bound running gaits (generally with an extended floating phase), which can be correlated with their short limbs and long and the distinctive morphology of their flexible axial skeleton. In these gaits the forelimbs have a poor role in the propulsion (which is mostly generated by the lumbar region and hind limbs) but are specialized in enduring the forces of reaction of the substrate during landing in the beginning of the stance phase."
- Discussion (on brachioradialis in otters): "In otters, the great mass of the muscle and its extreme position of proximal origin increases to the maximum the mechanical advantage of it, turning into the most powerful flexor of the elbow articulation. This configuration is consistent with the obtainment of a hydrodynamic silhouette, maintaining the limbs close to the body."
- Discussion (on the rotator-cuff reduction): "The intrinsic musculature in weasel-like mustelids represents only between the 35% and 40% of the musculature of the forelimb… and, differing from cursorials species and other generalized arctoids, weasel-like mustelids show a low development of the shoulder musculature relative both to the total musculature of the forelimb… as well as the intrinsic musculature alone, representing less than 30% of the latter."

## Attachment scoring pass

This paper had been mined for **names** — most of the therian forelimb occurrence
labels cite it — but not for **attachments**. Twenty-five Theria occurrences
carried `sources: ["ercoli-etal-2014"]` with no `attachments` block, which is the
largest single block of that kind the dataset has had.

**Theria went from 38% to 69% scored**, level with Caudata, and the dataset
overall from 43% to 47%. Region effects: forearm 31→45%, hand 24→36%,
pectoral 45→52%, arm 55→58%.

Scored: `protractor-pectoralis`, `serratus-anterior`, `rhomboideus`,
`levator-scapulae`, `deltoideus-scapularis`, `scapulohumeralis-anterior`,
`teres-major`, `brachialis`, `brachioradialis`, `supinator`, `anconeus`,
`epitrochleoanconeus`, `flexor-carpi-ulnaris`, `flexor-carpi-radialis`,
`pronator-teres`, `pronator-quadratus`, `palmaris-longus`,
`extensor-antebrachii-carpi-ulnaris`, `extensor-digitorum`,
`abductor-pollicis-longus`, `flexor-digitorum-longus`, `lumbricales`,
`abductor-pollicis-brevis`, `abductor-digiti-minimi`, `flexores-breves-profundi`.

Three things worth carrying forward:

**The entepicondylar foramen locates an origin.** The epitrochleoanconeus arises
from the humerus *distal to the caudal opening of the entepicondylar foramen* — a
carnivoran feature, and a precise bony landmark for a muscle that in humans is
inconstant. Recorded in `attachmentNote`; the foramen has no element of its own.

**The teres minor was absent on both sides of one of six specimens.** An
intraspecific absence in the taxon this dataset treats as the therian column. The
occurrence is scored present; the absence is in the note.

**Several origins are fascia, raphe or ligament rather than bone** — the whole
trapezius, the supinator (elbow capsule and lateral ligaments), palmaris longus
(palmar fascia), lumbricales (the profundus tendons). Those muscles leave nothing
for a fossil reconstruction to find, which is a fact about the method and not a
gap in the scoring.

Not scored from this paper: `costocoracoideus`, whose Theria occurrence is named
*costoscapularis* from Abdala & Diogo and which Table 3 does not describe under
that name.

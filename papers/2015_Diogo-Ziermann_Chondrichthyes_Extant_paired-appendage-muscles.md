## Full citation

Diogo, R. & Ziermann, J.M. 2015. Muscles of Chondrichthyan Paired Appendages: Comparison With Osteichthyans, Deconstruction of the Fore–Hindlimb Serial Homology Dogma, and New Insights on the Evolution of the Vertebrate Neck. *The Anatomical Record* 298: 513–530. Published online 10 September 2014 (DOI 10.1002/ar.23047).

## Question

Two linked questions:

1. What is the plesiomorphic chondrichthyan muscular configuration of the pectoral and pelvic appendages (including the girdle-head interface via the cucullaris)?
2. Are the fore- and hindlimb muscles of extant tetrapods actually serial homologues — i.e., was there an ancestral duplication of appendage muscles in early gnathostomes that produced two originally similar sets of muscles which then diverged? Or is the striking forelimb/hindlimb similarity the result of convergence in selected tetrapod regions (the "similarity bottleneck" of Diogo et al. 2013)?

## Taxa and material

First study to dissect paired-appendage muscles in representatives of all three major extant chondrichthyan clades:

- **Holocephali**: *Hydrolagus colliei* (spotted ratfish), three adult females (Ward's Natural Science).
- **Selachii**: *Squalus acanthias* (spiny dogfish), three juvenile males (Mount Desert Island Biological Laboratory).
- **Batoidea**: *Leucoraja erinacea* (little skate), three juvenile females (MDIBL, Union College).

All nine specimens consistent within species. Cladogram (Fig. 2) maps character transitions onto Holocephali / Elasmobranchii (Selachii + Batoidea) / Chondrichthyes / Osteichthyes / Gnathostomata.

## Time frame

Extant, with explicit comparison to Devonian *Parayunnanolepis* antiarch (Zhu et al. 2012) to constrain the first pelvic+pectoral-bearing fish.

## Methods

- Fresh/preserved dissection of all muscles attaching to pectoral and pelvic fins and their girdles in each species. Muscle action inferred from fiber direction + topology.
- Unified Diogo & Abdala 2010 nomenclature applied across all vertebrates; synonyms (Walker, Jarvik, Marinelli, Macesic, Kajiura, Didier, Anderson) given in text.
- Character mapping onto the gnathostome cladogram (Fig. 2) with proposed plesiomorphic states at Chondrichthyes, Gnathostomata, and Chondrichthyes-internal nodes.
- Literature synthesis across Edgeworth 1935, Kesteven 1942, Miyake et al. 1992, Didier 1987/1995, Macesic & Kajiura 2010, and the Diogo/Abdala series on osteichthyan muscle homology.

## Findings

1. **The plesiomorphic chondrichthyan pectoral configuration** has very few muscles:
   - **Cucullaris** (= head branchial muscle): a single continuous muscle from the epaxial fascia inserting onto both the pectoral girdle and the dorsal surfaces of all branchial arches. This is the primitive gnathostome condition. In batoids and holocephalans, the cucullaris has divided into bundles during ontogeny.
   - **Retractor latero-ventralis pectoralis** and **retractor mesio-ventralis pectoralis**: trunk muscles (epaxial/hypaxial origin, not branchial), present in batoids and holocephalans but not found in shark specimens here.
   - **Pterygialis cranialis**: a protractor of the pectoral fin that runs from the girdle (coracoid bar in *Squalus*) to the propterygium. Present in all three clades; anatomically similar to the osteichthyan arrector ventralis of neopterygians (*but independently acquired*).
   - **Adductor of the pectoral fin**: two bundles — superficial (from girdle to dorsal radial cartilage surfaces) and profundus (deep; inserts proximally on the fin).
   - **Abductor of the pectoral fin**: also superficial + deep bundles; originates on coracoid bar, inserts on ventral radial cartilage surfaces.
   - **Levators 2, 3, 4, (5)** of the pectoral fin in holocephalans (*Hydrolagus*); derived from body musculature, not true branchial muscles.

2. **The plesiomorphic chondrichthyan pelvic configuration** has even fewer muscles:
   - **Adductor** (superficial + deep bundles).
   - **Abductor** (proximal + distal bundles — anatomically different from the pectoral abductor, where bundles are superficial/deep).
   - **Protractor** of the pelvic fin: from pelvic girdle to propterygium. Acquired at the Chondrichthyes node, not at the Gnathostomata node.
   - Batoids and holocephalans also have extra pelvic muscles (four ventral, four dorsal in *Leucoraja*) tied to benthic locomotion (punting).

3. **Counts of muscles "corresponding" between fore- and hindlimb across vertebrates** (Fig. 10, the paper's key figure):
   - *Parayunnanolepis* (earliest fish with both appendages, ~413 Ma): paired appendages not anatomically similar.
   - Chondrichthyes: 2 muscles per appendage (adductor + abductor, with bundles).
   - Dipnoi: 2 muscles per appendage — "remarkably different" girdles.
   - *Ambystoma* (phylogenetically plesiomorphic tetrapod): 19 forelimb muscles "correspond" to hindlimb; **but there is no topologically equivalent pelvic-thigh muscle matching pectoral-arm muscles**.
   - *Xenopus*: 12 correspondences.
   - *Trachemys* (turtle): 13. *Alligator*: 14. Chicken: 9.
   - *Rattus*: 16. *Homo sapiens*: 19.
   - Interpretation: similarity between fore- and hindlimb is concentrated in the zeugopodium + autopodium (forearm/leg, hand/foot), not in the girdle or stylopodium. The autopodium is an evolutionary novelty where Hox redeployment produces a genuine "similarity bottleneck" via co-option of the same developmental modules.

4. **The serial homology dogma is rejected.** The authors conclude "all the numerous, and in many cases strikingly similar, muscles of the tetrapod fore- and hindlimb were undoubtedly acquired independently (homoplasically) at some stage during the evolutionary transitions that occurred from early gnathostomes to tetrapods." The forelimb–hindlimb enigma (Diogo et al. 2013) is explained by convergent co-option of shared developmental toolkits, not by an ancestral muscle-set duplication.

5. **Implications for vertebrate neck evolution.** Three transitions identified:
   - **Ancestral gnathostome / Chondrichthyes**: single cucullaris from head/epaxial fascia to both branchial arches + pectoral girdle. Head and girdle muscularly continuous.
   - **Osteichthyes (bony fish)**: cucullaris subdivides into **protractor pectoralis** (to pectoral girdle only) + **levatores arcuum branchialium** (to branchial arches only). Still girdle-attached to head via protractor pectoralis. Levatores arcuum branchialium lost at the base of Amniota.
   - **Amniota**: protractor pectoralis gives rise to **trapezius** and **sternocleidomastoideus** — the neck muscles. The pectoral girdle is now anatomically and functionally more independent from head movements; the amniote neck emerges by liberation of the head from the girdle.

6. **The head-branchial origin of the pectoral girdle.** The fact that the cucullaris connects both branchial arches and the pectoral girdle in the plesiomorphic chondrichthyan condition supports the view that the pectoral girdle origin is developmentally and anatomically tied to branchial-arch evolution (Gillis, Dahn & Shubin 2009 — "shared developmental mechanisms pattern the gill arch and paired fin skeletons"). The pectoral girdle is head-derived in origin; only later in tetrapod evolution does it become functionally independent.

7. **Pterygialis cranialis / arrector ventralis is not homologous across gnathostomes.** Despite anatomical similarity, the chondrichthyan pterygialis cranialis and the neopterygian arrector ventralis were independently acquired (two evolutionary steps, chondrichthyan + neopterygian). Sarcopterygians, cladistians, chondrosteans lack the muscle — more parsimonious to treat as convergent acquisition than triple loss.

- **Fig. 1**: the "textbook" serial-homology-plus-divergence scheme the paper sets out to deconstruct. Useful classroom setup figure for "here's the dogma, now let's take it apart."
- **Fig. 2**: simplified cladogram with character transitions. The reference map for what was acquired/modified at each chondrichthyan node.
- **Figs. 3–9**: labeled dissection photographs of all three species at pectoral and pelvic girdles. These are the best-annotated published photos of chondrichthyan paired-appendage muscles.
- **Fig. 10**: the headline figure — counts of muscles "corresponding" between fore- and hindlimbs from *Parayunnanolepis* through *Homo sapiens*. The strongest visual argument that similarity concentrates in the autopodium + zeugopodium and is convergent, not inherited.

## Limitations

- n = 3 per species, one species per clade; limited sampling within Selachii (one shark) and Batoidea (one skate).
- Juvenile specimens for *Squalus* and *Leucoraja* — some muscle differentiation may still be ongoing, particularly the cucullaris bundles.
- Character-state assignments at ancestral nodes depend on a small set of exemplars; the "plesiomorphic chondrichthyan" state is inferred from three taxa, not a dense comparative sample.
- The serial homology deconstruction leans heavily on the Diogo/Abdala unifying nomenclature — if you accept different homology calls at the osteichthyan side of the tree, the muscle-count correspondences shift.
- The cucullaris innervation argument (CNX in chondrichthyans, CNXI + CNX derivatives in amniotes) rests on older literature that often assigned innervation a priori rather than tracing nerves directly in every dissection.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

- The definitive recent account of the **chondrichthyan paired-appendage muscle plan** — adductor + abductor (± bundles), pterygialis cranialis / protractor, cucullaris, and little else. Compare directly to Huber, Soares & de Carvalho 2011 for the cranial-muscle half of the same taxonomic story.
- Pair Fig. 10 with the forelimb–hindlimb lecture. The claim that textbook "serial homology" is actually convergence concentrated in the distal limb is non-obvious to students and worth a class session. Works well alongside Cole et al. 2011 (two modes of appendicular muscle formation) and Diogo et al. 2009 (sarcopterygian pectoral muscles).
- For the **vertebrate neck lecture**: the three-step cucullaris → protractor pectoralis → trapezius/sternocleidomastoideus pathway is a clean evolutionary-morphology narrative. Shows that "having a neck" is a muscular liberation event, not just a cervical-vertebrae count.
- For **fin-to-limb transition**: supports the Gillis, Dahn & Shubin 2009 view that the pectoral girdle is developmentally descended from branchial-arch patterning. Use the cucullaris continuity as the evolutionary evidence for the developmental claim.
- Classroom exercise: hand students Fig. 10 and the Diogo & Abdala 2010 muscle table, ask them to score fore/hindlimb correspondences in their favorite tetrapod, and debate whether the pattern they find is "homology" or "convergent similarity via shared developmental toolkit."

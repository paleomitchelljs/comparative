# Burch (2014) — Complete forelimb myology of *Tawa hallae*

## Citation

Burch SH. 2014. Complete forelimb myology of the basal theropod dinosaur *Tawa
hallae* based on a novel robust muscle reconstruction method. *Journal of Anatomy*
225: 271–297. doi:10.1111/joa.12216

## Question

What was the complete forelimb musculature of an early theropod — not just the
shoulder, which is as far as previous phylogenetic reconstructions had reached,
but the antebrachium and the intrinsic manual muscles as well? Those had gone
unstudied because archosaur homologies in the distal limb were unsettled, and
they are the muscles that decide what a hand could do.

## Taxa and material

*Tawa hallae*, Late Triassic of New Mexico.

The bracket under it is unusually broad. Attachment data for **41 avian species
across 26 family-level clades, four crocodylians, six lepidosaurs and six
turtles**, mostly from published myology, supplemented by her own dissections of
*Bubo virginianus*, *Caprimulgus carolinensis*, *Megaceryle alcyon* and **two
forelimbs of adult *Struthio camelus***.

## Methods — the reason this column is scorable at all

Ancestral states reconstructed by **maximum likelihood in Mesquite** over a
consensus phylogeny, then read against *Tawa*'s own osteology. That produces a
number rather than a judgement for every attachment, and the inference levels are
defined on it:

| Level | Means |
|---|---|
| **I** | Proportional probability > 0.50 at *both* nodes bracketing Dinosauria — Aves and Archosauria |
| **II** | > 0.50 at one of the two |
| **III** | Neither |
| prime (I′, II′, III′) | No osteological evidence on the bone for the state |

Level II is the minimum she will reconstruct from, and a prime level ranks below
a non-prime of the same level but above a non-prime of a lower one.

**This is why the stem-theropod column arrives well scored rather than at the half
or two-thirds a new taxon usually starts on.** Both halves of its bracket —
Crocodylia and Aves — are scored columns in this dataset already, so nothing had
to be inferred from a composite.

## Scored

38 occurrence rows on *Tawa hallae*, across the pectoral girdle, arm, forearm and
hand. **Every row is `inferred`**: this is a hypothesis about an animal nobody
dissected, and each `attachmentNote` says which inference level it rests on and
which correlate, if any, is actually on the bone. Several rest on scars found in
*Herrerasaurus* or *Sanjuansaurus* rather than in *Tawa* itself.

**Four rows are deliberately left without attachments, and each names a different
kind of failure:**

- **`lumbricales`** — present almost certainly (they are in every outgroup with a
  normal manus), but Burch calls reconstructing their morphology too speculative
  to attempt. The variation is in exactly the two things an attachment row records.
- **`intermetacarpales` and `dorsometacarpales`** — the interossei, whose
  attachments vary faster than the clades do.
- **`contrahentes-digitorum`** — scored `uncertain` rather than `inferred`, and
  the uncertainty is about *presence*. Burch treats this group as an assortment of
  smaller muscles varying between and sometimes within clades, and gives a positive
  reason to expect *Tawa* had lost them.

## Findings worth carrying

**The basal theropod shoulder is not the dromaeosaurid shoulder.** It resembles
that of basal ornithischians and sauropodomorphs instead. Greater development of
the supracoracoideus and deltoideus in theropods correlates with stronger shoulder
movement and an emphasis on apprehending relatively large prey, and the
antebrachium and manus support the same reading — high excursion and a morphology
suited to powerful digital flexion.

**She argues with her own sources in print, and the disagreements are recorded
rather than resolved.** Against **Jasinoski et al. (2006)** on the deltoid origins,
on the supracoracoideus insertion and on the presence of coracobrachialis longus;
against **Diogo & Abdala (2010)** on the homology of the avian deltoideus minor and
ulnimetacarpalis dorsalis; against **Meers (2003)** on which crocodylian muscle
carries which name.

**Three rows are worth reading for method rather than for content:**

- **`abductor-digiti-minimi` exists because she dissected two ostriches.** No
  muscle answering the description had been reported in an avian manus, which would
  have left the archosaur bracket one-sided. She found a well-developed one in both
  *Struthio* specimens.
- **`scapulohumeralis-anterior` is reconstructed against its own bracket.** The
  muscle is absent in extant crocodylians and in ratites; it is restored from other
  birds and from lepidosaurs. The bracket that makes the rest of this column good is
  the one thing that does not support that row.
- **`flexores-breves-superficiales` is reconstructed against her own plesiomorphy
  argument, and she says so.** In lepidosaurs and turtles these bellies arise from
  the annular ligament over the carpals; that ligament is absent in both extant
  archosaur clades, whose wrists are modified. *Tawa*'s wrist is closer to a
  lepidosaur's, so the ligament was probably there.

## Limitations

Everything here is a reconstruction, and the paper is scrupulous about saying so
— which is why the rows can be scored honestly. Two structural cautions:

- **Osteological evidence is thin in the manus**, so prime-level inferences
  concentrate there.
- **A bracket can be unanimous and still be wrong about a stem taxon.** Several
  attachments reconstruct unequivocally at the archosaur node while both extant
  clades have modified the region — the wrist especially.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The clearest worked example in the corpus of the extant phylogenetic bracket done
quantitatively rather than impressionistically: every attachment carries a
probability, an inference level, and a statement of whether the bone itself shows
anything. Pair with **Molnar & Diogo (2021)** for the confidence vocabulary and
**Molnar et al. (2018)** for the same logic applied at the other end of the
tetrapod stem.

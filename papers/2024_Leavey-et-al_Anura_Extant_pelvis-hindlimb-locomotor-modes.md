# Leavey et al. (2024) — Anuran pelvis and hindlimb muscle anatomy by locomotor mode

## Citation

Leavey A, et al. 2024. Comparative muscle anatomy of the anuran pelvis and hindlimb
in relation to locomotor mode. *Journal of Anatomy*.

## Question

Frogs have a highly conserved body plan and a diverse array of locomotor modes,
which makes them the right group for asking whether **anatomical complexity is a
prerequisite for functional complexity**.

## Taxa and material

**30 frog species across five locomotor modes**, digitally dissected by diffusible
iodine contrast-enhanced microCT (diceCT) — the largest digital comparative
musculoskeletal analysis in any vertebrate clade at the time of writing.

## The finding this dataset has to carry

**Bone lengths and pelvic crest sizes are generally *not* reliable predictors of
muscle size**, and they find many-to-one form-function mapping: different anatomical
configurations produce the same functional outcome.

That constrains what an osteological correlate can be used for, and the constraint
is now recorded in `METHODS.md`. **A correlate locates an attachment. It does not
size the muscle that made it.** A diagram that scaled muscle bands to bone
dimensions would be asserting something these data reject, which is why roadmap
phase 4 specifies a schematic rather than a scaled figure.

## Scored

3 occurrence rows — iliofemoralis, extensor iliotibialis, ischioflexorius — on
*Discoglossus pictus*.

The rows are thin relative to the paper's scope because the data are largely in
figures and in the comparative analysis rather than in per-species attachment
statements.

## Limitations

- **Not a row source at scale.** 28 origin/insertion mentions across the paper.
  Its contribution here is a methodological constraint and a few rows, not a column.
- **diceCT resolves muscle volumes well and attachment *sites* less well** than a
  dissection that can lift a tendon.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The right paper for the moment a student proposes reading muscle size off a bone.
Pair with **Pereyra et al. (2019)**, which shows histologically that the same muscle
on the same bone leaves Sharpey's fibres in some turtle species and none in others
— together they bracket what a scar can and cannot tell you.

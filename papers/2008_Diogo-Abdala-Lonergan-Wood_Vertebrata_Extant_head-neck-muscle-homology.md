# Diogo et al. (2008) — From fish to modern humans: head and neck musculature

## Citation

Diogo R, Abdala V, Lonergan N, Wood BA. 2008. From fish to modern humans —
comparative anatomy, homologies and evolution of the head and neck musculature.
*Journal of Anatomy* 213: 391–424. doi:10.1111/j.1469-7580.2008.00953.x

## Question

The second half of a two-part study. Diogo (2008, *Animal Biology* — a separate
source in this repository, keyed `diogo-2008`) covered the actinopterygians; this
paper covers the sarcopterygians, and specifically how the head and neck muscles
changed across the transitions from sarcopterygian fish through non-mammalian
tetrapods to monotremes, therians and modern humans.

## What it is

**Four homology schemes, one per muscle group** — mandibular, hyoid, branchial and
pharyngeal/laryngeal, and hypobranchial — each running across representative
sarcopterygian taxa from fish to humans. Built from the authors' own dissections of
sarcopterygian fish, amphibians, reptiles, monotremes and therian mammals (rodents,
tree-shrews, colugos and primates including humans), compared against the literature.

## Findings

**Modern humans have fewer mandibular and true branchial muscles than tree-shrews,
rats, monotremes or lizards.** The pharyngeal musculature gains muscles at the
transition to therian mammals, but not again at the transition to higher primates
and humans. The hypobranchial count is nearly constant within therians, with humans
having slightly more. The laryngeal and facial counts in humans exceed most other
therians, and humans possess laryngeal and facial muscles absent in the majority of
other mammals — which the authors read as corroborating the role of vocal
communication and facial expression in human evolution.

## What it gave this dataset

**Three homology arguments, cited on the records they bear on.** The paper is not a
row source and is not scored as one; what it supplies is provenance for the arch-2
story the cranial records tell.

**The reptilian depressor mandibulae becomes several mammalian muscles.** Against
Edgeworth (1935), who derived the monotreme styloideus and the therian stylohyoideus
from the interhyoideus, they follow Huber (1930a) and Saban (1968, 1971): the
monotreme **styloideus and stapedius**, and the therian **stylohyoideus, digastricus
posterior, jugulohyoideus, stapedius** and possibly mandibulo-auricularis, all
correspond to the depressor mandibulae of reptiles such as *Timon*. Gasser's (1967)
developmental study supports this from the other end — in humans the digastricus
posterior, stapedius and stylohyoideus derive from one anlage.

**The stapedius is a depressor mandibulae derivative**, not a middle-ear novelty. It
followed the hyomandibula into the ear rather than being built there, which is the
muscular counterpart of the skeletal sequence on `hyomandibula-stapes`.

**The mammalian facial muscles run through the interhyoideus field.** They support
Lightoller (1942) and Saban (1971): the reptilian sphincter colli, itself an
interhyoideus derivative, corresponds to the mammalian sphincter colli superficialis
and profundus and their derivatives. The older view that the mammalian facial
muscles were *de novo* structures has been abandoned.

Cited on `depressor-mandibulae`, `stapedius` and `interhyoideus`. It carries
`homologyScope: true`, but changes no record's authority: Ziermann & Diogo (2019) is
more recent and already holds most of the cranial records.

## Limitations

**The tables are diagrams, not tables.** All four homology schemes are arrow
diagrams — black arrows for the best-supported hypothesis, grey for alternatives
that some of the data support — and they do not extract as text. Only the numbered
footnotes below each one do, which is where the three arguments above were read. **A
proper pass on this source means reading the four figures**, and it would be the
single most useful thing anyone could do for the cranial column, which this
repository's audit found to be its coarsest.

**No attachments.** Fewer than two origin/insertion mentions per page. This is a
scheme of correspondences, not a description of where anything attaches — the
attachments for any taxon in it have to come from the descriptive source for that
taxon.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The paper that lets a student put a shark's jaw muscle and a human's stapedius on
one page and say why they belong together. Its most teachable result is the negative
one: **a human head has fewer mandibular and branchial muscles than a rat's, a
tree-shrew's, an echidna's or a lizard's**, which is a useful thing to have on hand
the first time someone assumes human anatomy is the elaborated end of a series.

Pair with **Diogo (2008, *Animal Biology*)** for the actinopterygian half of the same
study, with **Diogo et al. (2009)** for the facial muscles in detail, with
**Anderson (2008)** for the gnathostome cranial homologies from the other direction,
and with **Werneburg (2011)** on why comparative cranial nomenclature is in the state
it is.

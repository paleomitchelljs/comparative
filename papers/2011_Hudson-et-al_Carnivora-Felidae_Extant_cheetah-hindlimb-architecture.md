# Hudson et al. (2011) — Functional anatomy of the cheetah hindlimb

## Citation

Hudson PE, Corr SA, Payne-Davis RC, Clancy SN, Lane E, Wilson AM. 2011. Functional
anatomy of the cheetah (*Acinonyx jubatus*) hindlimb. *Journal of Anatomy* 218:
363–374. Companion to the forelimb paper (218: 375–385).

## Question

As for the forelimb: what anatomy accounts for 29 m s⁻¹ in a cheetah against 17 in
a greyhound of similar mass? Same protocol — bone length, mass and mid-shaft
diameter, plus muscle mass, fascicle length, pennation angle and moment arm.

## Findings worth carrying

**The result is counterintuitive and the authors say so.** Cheetahs have a
*smaller* volume of hip extensor musculature than greyhounds. Their proposal is
that the cheetah powers acceleration with its **back musculature** instead, which
moves the explanation out of the limb entirely.

**An extremely powerful psoas**, which they suggest resists the pitching moment
around the hip that fast acceleration generates.

**Hindlimb bones proportionally longer and heavier.**

## Scored

6 records carrying architecture — iliofemoralis, extensor iliotibialis,
femorotibialis, adductor femoris, ischioflexorius, gastrocnemius. As with the
forelimb paper, the `architecture` block is the cheetah's while the attachments on
those records belong to the named species.

One row carries a useful comparative observation: the same muscle, with the same
nerve and the same attachments, becomes the principal pelvic stabiliser during
single-limb support once the limb is parasagittal. Posture changes the job without
changing the anatomy.

## Limitations

Same as the forelimb paper: two species, one comparison, and architecture rather
than attachment — 27 origin/insertion mentions in the paper. Not a row source.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The single best illustration in the corpus that locomotor performance is not read
off the limb. The fastest land mammal has *less* hip extensor muscle than a
greyhound, and the proposed answer is the axial system — which is the argument for
why the axial records exist at all. Pair with **Schilling (2011)**.

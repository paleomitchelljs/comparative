# Molnar & Diogo 2021 — Evolution, Homology, and Development of Tetrapod Limb Muscles

## Citation

Molnar, J.L. & Diogo, R. 2021. Evolution, Homology, and Development of Tetrapod Limb Muscles. *Diversity* 13(8): 393. https://doi.org/10.3390/d13080393. MDPI open-access review. Received 23 June, accepted 19 August, published 21 August 2021. 24 pp. Academic Editors Raul E. Diaz and Octávio Mateus.

## Format note

Synthetic review, not primary data. Integrates: (1) 100+ years of fossil-tetrapod muscle-reconstruction literature (Watson 1917, Gregory & Camp 1918, Romer 1922, Miner 1925, Haines 1939, Holmes 1977, Sumida 1989, Panchen, Ahlberg 2011, Bishop 2014, Molnar et al. 2017/2018, etc.); (2) extant phylogenetic bracket methodology history; (3) comparative developmental studies (authors' own work on axolotls, frogs, chameleons, humans); (4) unresolved homology controversies (deltoid group, teres major, subcoracoscapularis, radial extensors, caudifemoralis, brachioradialis, coracoradialis). Senior-authored by Diogo + postdoc Molnar — represents the synthetic endpoint of their 2014–2020 primary-data paper series already in the library.

## Verbatim quotes (extracted first)

**Thesis + rationale:**

> "Since the early 1900s, researchers have attempted to unravel the origin and evolution of tetrapod limb muscles using a combination of comparative anatomy, phylogeny, and development. The methods for reconstructing soft tissues in extinct animals have been refined over time as our ability to determine muscle homology and phylogenetic relationships between tetrapods has improved."

> "Since many muscles do not leave osteological correlates, muscle reconstruction in extinct animals is largely based on anatomy and development in extant animals. While muscle anatomy in extant tetrapods is quite conservative, the homologies of certain muscles between taxonomic groups are still uncertain."

> "The study of muscles has too often been neglected in recent decades, especially in a broad phylogenetic context that spans all major tetrapod groups. Exceptional comparative myological studies were conducted in the late 1800s and early 1900s by authors such as M. Fürbringer, H. Gadow, J. P. McMurrich, W. K. Gregory, A. S. Romer, F. H., Edgeworth, and G. M. Humphry. However, these authors lacked crucial information about the phylogenetic relationships of tetrapods and developmental patterning of muscles provided by modern molecular, genetic, and developmental studies."

**Extant phylogenetic bracket formalization:**

> "'Extant phylogenetic bracket' describes the relationship between a fossil taxon and its two closest extant sister groups, and the EPB approach is a method of phylogenetic inference based on this relationship [7]. For example, an EPB of the stem mammal *Dimetrodon* must include at least a crown reptile and a mammal (Figure 1). The EPB approach involves three steps: first, determine a causal relationship between a preserved and an unpreserved attribute; for example, the cnemial crest in extant amniotes is produced by the quadriceps tendon that attaches to it. Second, form a hypothesis that this association is homologous among the extant taxa of interest; for example, that a common ancestor of extant amniotes had a cnemial crest produced by a quadriceps tendon. Finally, test this hypothesis by looking for the osteological correlate in the fossil ancestors of extant amniotes (e.g., *Dimetrodon, Cynognathus, Captorhinus*)."

> "These methods can provide evidence of muscle attachment areas but cannot reliably predict muscle sizes or architecture [16]."

> "Phylogenetically based reconstruction is most straightforward in clades with a good fossil record and at least two morphologically similar bracket taxa, such as human ancestors. It is much more challenging to reconstruct muscles in animals with no morphologically similar extant analogue and/or highly divergent bracket taxa, such as stem tetrapods and non-avian dinosaurs."

**Key historical claim about Romer:**

> "It is impossible to overstate the importance of A. S. Romer's contributions to comparative anatomy, functional morphology, and paleontology, as many writers have pointed out (e.g., [17,25]). Romer's [1] manuscript on the appendicular muscles of stem amniotes and stem mammals built upon the work of Watson [23] and Gregory and Camp [6], and it aimed to 'bridge the gap' in limb structure between reptiles and mammals [1], p. 519. Notably, this manuscript described the tetrahedral structure underlying the tetrapod humerus, a great achievement that enabled the identification of homologous regions, processes, and foramina across amniotes and lissamphibians."

> "In addition to venturing outside of crown Amniota, this manuscript was also the first to propose homology between the limb muscles of tetrapods and the fin muscles of fish [1]. The author followed Sewertzoff [26] in dividing tetrapod limb muscles into dorsal and ventral groups, which are largely the same as the ones recovered in modern developmental studies. However, unlike Sewertzoff [26] and modern studies, Romer [1] classed the deltoid and procoracohumeralis and their derivatives as ventral muscles."

**Stem-tetrapod reconstruction as Lagerstätte + EPB problem:**

> "Stem tetrapods present a problem for phylogenetically based muscle reconstruction because of the vast morphological gulf between the appendages of tetrapods and those of fish. This gap makes it difficult to establish hypotheses of homology between appendicular muscles in the extant bracket taxa."

> "The first reasonably complete muscle reconstruction of stem tetrapods with limbs was produced by Bishop [9] for the Early Carboniferous whatcheeriid *Ossinodus*. In the absence of detailed muscle homology hypotheses, the author was able to reconstruct all the forelimb muscles in this fossil because of its extraordinary preservation. Scars of what appear to be individual muscle fascicles cover distinct areas of the bones and seem to indicate direct fleshy attachments, which seldom leave recognizable osteological correlates [9]. This extensive scarring allowed the author to use *Ossinodus* as a bracket taxon, similar to the Lagerstätten that preserve soft tissues [7]."

> "Molnar and colleagues [10,11] attempted the first detailed muscle reconstructions in stem tetrapods using the extant phylogenetic bracket method. Building upon the work of Romer [1,19], they reconstructed forelimb and hindlimb muscles in the stem tetrapods *Acanthostega* (Devonian) and *Pederpes* (Early Carboniferous), as well as a Devonian tetrapodomorph fish (*Eusthenopteron*), based on extant coelacanths, lungfish, salamanders, and 'lizards' [10,11]. They posited that the dorsal and ventral muscle masses of the fish fin underwent both proximo-distal and anterior-posterior segmentation over the fin-limb transition, passing through an intermediate stage that somewhat resembled the fins of extant Australian lungfish and coelacanths."

> "Musculoskeletal models of the three stem tetrapods (including *Eusthenopteron*) based on these reconstructions revealed increased muscle moment arms for humeral retractor muscles across the fin-limb transition, implying that the earliest steps in tetrapod forelimb evolution were related to aquatic limb-substrate interactions rather than body support [12]."

**Summary of deltoid-group controversy (Fig. 2, the teaching figure):**

> "Despite different evolutionary hypotheses and different naming conventions, the muscle reconstructions [of *Acanthostega*, *Ossinodus*, *Eryops*, *Diadectes*, *Captorhinus*, *Dimetrodon*, *Cynognathus*] are almost identical. Muscle names follow Bishop [9], with the names given in the original publications in parentheses."

**Development — "in-out" mechanism + limb-muscle plasticity:**

> "Recent comparative development studies by our lab and others, building on the work of 20th century anatomists (e.g., [26,38,45,50,51,56,57]), have revealed general developmental patterns. For instance, our work on the development of fore- and hindlimb muscles in salamanders (Figure 3A–C) [58], frogs [59], reptilians (e.g., chameleons, in review), and mammals (humans; [60]) support the 'in-out' mechanistic hypothesis, sensu Valasek et al. [61]. A similar idea was proposed more than a century ago, by Sewertzoff [26]. According to this hypothesis, the myogenic cells that form the superficial girdle muscles that attach to the humerus first migrate 'in' from the somites into the limb bud and subsequently extend 'out' from the limb bud toward the body wall [61]. In contrast, the deep girdle muscles ('axial pectoral muscles') are induced by the forelimb field that promotes myotomal extension directly from the somites."

> "Although muscle patterning is remarkably conservative among tetrapods [50,64], the tempo and mode of muscle morphogenesis vary among taxa. In regeneration studies of the salamander forelimb, the formation and differentiation of the muscles follow proximo-distal and preaxial-postaxial gradients (e.g., [65–67]). However, Diogo et al.'s [67] study using transgenic animals that express green fluorescent protein also found a marked ventro-dorsal gradient during the regeneration of at least some axolotl forearm muscles."

> "A postaxial-preaxial morphogenetic pattern is also found in the limb skeletons of other non-urodele tetrapod groups and in the limb muscles in at least some of these groups."

> "The remarkable plasticity implied by the existence of different morphogenetic gradients among tetrapod taxa makes it difficult to discern plesiomorphic states and general morphogenetic patterns [37]."

**Fore-hindlimb serial-homology rejection:**

> "The idea that the pectoral and pelvic appendages, like vertebrae, arose by duplication of an ancestral structure (serial homology hypothesis) has recently been called into question. These challenges rest on comparative anatomical studies of adult appendicular muscles among numerous vertebrate taxa and on a review of other lines of evidence, including paleontology, functional morphology, Evo-Devo, and genetics [80–84]. Diogo et al. [81] concluded that the enigmatic similarity between many forelimb and hindlimb structures ('fore-hindlimb enigma'), including muscles, was acquired during the 'fins-limbs transition' through the *derived* cooption of some similar genes for the development of the more distal parts of both the forelimb and the hindlimb. That is, while it is possible that the fore- and hindlimbs display 'deep homology' in the developmental sense (i.e., shared gene regulatory circuits [85]), they are not homologous in the morphological or phylogenetic sense."

## Question

Three coupled questions. (1) How have methods for reconstructing limb muscles in extinct tetrapods evolved from Watson 1917 to Molnar et al. 2017/2018, and what is the modern best practice (EPB)? (2) What do the recurring controversies (deltoid group, teres major, subcoracoscapularis, caudifemoralis, brachioradialis, scapulohumeralis anterior/posterior) actually turn on, and how do developmental + molecular data constrain them? (3) How does the tempo and mode of limb-muscle morphogenesis across tetrapod groups inform the origin of paired fins, the fin-to-limb transition, and the evolution of morphological complexity?

## Taxa covered (Fig. 1 + text)

- **Stem tetrapods**: *Acanthostega* (Devonian; Molnar et al. 2017, 2018), *Ichthyostega* (1985, 2017), *Crassigyrinus* (1985), Whatcheeriidae: *Ossinodus* (Bishop 2014), *Pederpes* (Molnar et al. 2017, 2018). Tetrapodomorph fish *Eusthenopteron* (Andrews & Westoll 1970; Molnar et al. 2017) also treated here.
- **Stem lissamphibians**: Temnospondyls *Eryops* (Miner 1925, 1939, 1977), *Labidosaurus* (in Romer 1922).
- **Stem amniotes**: Embolomeres *Archeria* (1922), *Eogyrinus* (1924); Diadectomorpha *Diadectes* (Romer 1922, 1924).
- **Stem reptiles** (captorhinids): *Captorhinus* (Holmes 1977), *Labidosaurus* (Romer 1922; Sumida 1989).
- **Stem mammals**: Ophiacodontidae *Varanosaurus* (Watson 1917), *Ophiacodon* (Romer 1939); Sphenacodontia *Dimetrodon* (Romer 1922); Cynodontia *Cynognathus* (Gregory & Camp 1918, Romer 1922, 1977), *Diademodon* (Watson 1917); Dinocephalia *Moschops* (Romer 1922).
- **Extant**: *Neoceratodus* (Australian lungfish), *Andrias* / *Ambystoma* (salamanders), *Sphenodon*, *Timon* / *Iguana* (lizards), *Alligator*, *Gallus* (birds), *Ornithorhynchus*, *Didelphis* / *Trichosurus* (marsupials), *Rattus*, *Homo*.

## Time frame

Phanerozoic. Fossil record from Early Devonian fish to modern humans.

## Methods

Review, not primary data. Three review tracks:

- **Historical methodology review** (Watson 1917 → Gregory & Camp 1918 → Romer 1922 → Miner 1925 → Haines 1939 → Holmes 1977 → Sumida 1989 → Witmer 1995 EPB formalization → Bishop 2014 → Molnar et al. 2017, 2018). Traces the progression from "primitive type" comparison (*Sphenodon* + *Echidna* as default brackets, before phylogenetic hypotheses) → compound-character recognition → EPB Level I/II/III inference.
- **Controversy-by-controversy treatment** (Section 2.7): deltoid group (Fig. 2 is the headline teaching figure, showing reconstructions of 7 fossil taxa from 6 different authors produced nearly identical musculature despite different evolutionary hypotheses + names); subcoracoscapularis presence/absence; teres major origin (latissimus-derived vs subcoracoscapularis-derived); radial extensors (3 vs 4 bundles, brachioradialis homology); caudifemoralis in stem reptiles (Romer 1922 reduction vs Sumida 1989 large insertion).
- **Developmental + morphogenetic gradient review**: Diogo et al. (axolotl GFP + salamander in-out mechanism), Diogo & Tanaka 2014 (Fig. 3 — transgenic axolotl), frog + chameleon + human. Discusses proximo-distal + preaxial-postaxial + ventro-dorsal morphogenetic gradients + their variable presence across tetrapod groups.

No tables. Figures: Fig. 1 (cladogram + dated reconstructions), Fig. 2 (7-taxon deltoid-group reconstruction comparison), Fig. 3 (axolotl GFP developmental + adult atlas), Fig. 4 (axolotl regeneration stages, not shown in my excerpts). 98 references.

## Findings (numbered)

1. **EPB is now the standard** for fossil soft-tissue reconstruction, formalized by Witmer 1995, but already implicit in Miner 1925 (two-extant-outgroup + causal-hypothesis structure). Muscle reconstructions are Level I–III inferences about attachment areas only; muscle *size* + architecture cannot be reliably inferred from bone alone.
2. **Stem tetrapods are EPB-hard** because of the fish-tetrapod morphological gulf. Two solutions emerged: (a) *Ossinodus*-as-Lagerstätte (Bishop 2014) — exceptional muscle-scar preservation allowed muscle reconstruction without strict EPB; (b) *Acanthostega* + *Pederpes* EPB with coelacanths + lungfish + salamanders + lizards (Molnar et al. 2017, 2018). Both approaches converged on similar anatomy — fish dorsal/ventral muscle masses underwent simultaneous proximo-distal + anterior-posterior segmentation in the fin-limb transition, passing through an intermediate stage resembling extant lungfish + coelacanth fins.
3. **Earliest stages of forelimb evolution were about aquatic limb-substrate interaction, not body support** — musculoskeletal models (Molnar et al. 2018) of *Eusthenopteron* + *Acanthostega* + *Pederpes* show increasing humeral-retractor moment arms across the transition, not increased support-musculature capacity.
4. **Deltoid group evolution**: reconstructions of *Acanthostega*, *Ossinodus*, *Eryops*, *Diadectes*, *Captorhinus*, *Dimetrodon*, *Cynognathus* from Romer (1922), Miner (1925), Holmes (1977), Gregory & Camp (1918), Bishop (2014), Molnar et al. (2017, 2018) — **despite different evolutionary hypotheses + different names (scapulohumeralis anterior vs procoracohumeralis; single vs split deltoideus), the actual reconstructions are almost identical** (Fig. 2). The controversy is largely nomenclatural + causal-hypothesis-dependent, not anatomically substantive.
5. **Subcoracoscapularis in stem tetrapods + stem mammals**: ambiguous. Development places it as originally scapular-only in stem tetrapods per Diogo & Tanaka 2014 + axolotl evidence; only within amniotes did the coracoid head appear (Squamate evidence). *Dimetrodon* reconstruction oscillates (Watson 1917: subcoracoid + scapular; Romer 1922: scapular only; Holmes 1977: both).
6. **Teres major was NOT ancestrally amniote**. Reptilian "teres major" and mammalian teres major are non-homologous: reptilian teres major develops from the latissimus dorsi mass + shares latissimus innervation; mammalian teres major develops from the subcoracoscapularis mass + shares subscapular-nerve innervation. Independent acquisition in mammals + reptiles.
7. **Radial extensor (extensor carpi radialis)** has 3 bundles (superficialis, intermedius, profundus) + supinator longus/"tractor radii" in most amphibians + reptiles. The three bundles are homologous with mammalian extensors carpi radialis longus + brevis + supinator (brevis). Whether the intermedius bundle is homologous with the mammalian brachioradialis is disputed — some lizards + turtles have intermedius innervated by the inferior brachial nerve (ventral), while brachioradialis is radial-nerve-innervated (dorsal). Authors tentatively side with Miner 1925's 3-bundle *Eryops* reconstruction.
8. **Caudifemoralis in captorhinids is likely large** (Sumida 1989's reconstruction based on the more complete UCLA VP 3167), not reduced (Romer 1922) — consistent with extant-non-avian-reptile muscle proportions. But the attachment-size-to-muscle-size mapping is not 1:1.
9. **"In-out" mechanism of pectoral-girdle muscle development** is supported across salamanders, frogs, chameleons, humans. Myogenic cells that form the superficial girdle muscles migrate from the somites **into the limb bud first** + then extend outward onto the thoracic wall. Deep "axial pectoral" muscles come directly from the somites, induced by the forelimb field.
10. **Ontogenetic gradients are taxon-variable**, not universal. Axolotl forelimb has a ventro-dorsal gradient (Diogo et al., transgenic GFP); axolotl hindlimb has only proximo-distal + preaxial-postaxial gradients. Frog anterior-posterior gradient is reversed from salamander. **Morphogenetic plasticity** — not a fixed tetrapod patterning program.
11. **Radial-side hypertrophy predicted by preaxial-postaxial gradient hypothesis**: most tetrapods have more radial/tibial muscles than ulnar/fibular muscles, consistent with the ulnar muscles never reaching the radial muscles' developmental stage. Supported in axolotl hindlimb (two long tibial-side extensors vs one fibular-side extensor), chickens, humans.
12. **Fore-hindlimb serial homology is rejected** — the similarity between fore- and hindlimb muscles (the "fore-hindlimb enigma") is the result of *derived* co-option of shared gene regulatory networks (e.g., Hox, FGFs, RA, Shh) in distal appendage development, not ancestral duplication. Deep homology (developmental) ≠ morphological/phylogenetic homology (Diogo et al. 2013; Diogo & Ziermann 2015 in library).

## Limitations

- Review, not new primary data. Reader needs to pull Molnar et al. 2017 (*Anat. Rec.*), Molnar et al. 2018 (*Proc. R. Soc. B*), Bishop 2014 (*Earth Env. Sci. Trans. R. Soc. Edinburgh*), Diogo & Ziermann 2015 (*Anat. Rec.*, in library) for primary data.
- Fig. 2 deltoid-group synthesis is visually powerful but the pink/green/blue color assignments still reflect the authors' (Molnar + Diogo) homology hypothesis; readers who follow Romer's 1922 scapulohumeralis-from-subscapularis scheme would color things differently.
- Developmental section is authors' own-lab-heavy (axolotls + frogs + chameleons-in-review + humans). Independent labs' developmental data on other tetrapod taxa are cited but not synthesized.
- The paper explicitly says extant myological studies by Fürbringer, Gadow, McMurrich, Gregory, Romer, Edgeworth, Humphry are foundational but "lacked crucial information" about phylogeny + molecular data — a sympathetic framing that still leaves the 1920s–1930s reconstructions as anatomical baselines.
- EPB methodology is presented as the current standard but not discussed in its limits (e.g., EPB fails when both sister groups have lost a muscle via convergence; EPB cannot constrain muscle *size*; fossil osteological correlates can be ambiguous).
- **Weakness section (Controversies) is non-exhaustive** — the authors flag only those muscles that have been addressed in published fossil reconstructions, leaving many smaller muscles (humeroradialis, scapulohumeralis posterior details, pubotibialis, coracoradialis, femorofibularis, dorsoepitrochlearis) as "see references therein."
- MDPI *Diversity* is a relatively low-impact open-access venue; the review is not a Nature/Science scoped piece.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Four teaching uses. (1) **As the single best primer for extant phylogenetic bracketing** for a vertebrate-evolution course — Fig. 1 maps the whole tetrapod tree with reconstructed taxa, and the EPB step-by-step exposition (p. 2) is the cleanest textbook version. (2) **As the historiography of a single scientific problem** — 100 years of limb-muscle reconstruction compressed into one paper; assign as "how does a field mature?" reading, with Fig. 2 as the "despite all these methods, reconstructions converge" payoff image. (3) **As the fin-to-limb muscle evolution exhibit** — pairs naturally with Ahlberg 2018 + Molnar et al. 2018 (humeral retractor moment arm increase + aquatic-substrate origin). (4) **As the fore-hindlimb serial homology retraction** — the "enigma" framing motivates a class discussion of what "homology" means in developmental vs phylogenetic vs genetic senses (use with Diogo & Ziermann 2015 paired-appendage paper already in library). Pair with **Molnar et al. 2018** (pectoral muscles in fins-to-limbs transition — in library), **Diogo & Ziermann 2015** (paired-appendage muscles across chondrichthyans + rejection of fore-hindlimb serial homology — in library), **Ahlberg 2018** (tetrapod origin review — in library), **Miyashita 2015** (pharyngeal-arch distinctness), **Diogo 2008 + Diogo & Abdala 2010** (osteichthyan-sarcopterygian muscle coverage — in library), **Castiello & Brazeau 2018** (placoderm braincase EPB analog — in library), **Brazeau & Friedman 2014** (placoderm-acanthodian paraphyly + character hierarchy — in library), **Cole et al. 2011** (pelvic-fin muscle development — in library), **Crompton et al. 2018** (mammaliaform braincase synapsid-transition — in library), **Fahn-Lai et al. 2020** (tegu-opossum shoulder bracket — in library). **Pedagogic headline**: limb-muscle reconstruction is the EPB case study that shows how a 1920s problem becomes tractable only after both phylogeny (1980s) and developmental biology (2000s) arrive — comparative anatomy is a late-maturing science, and the synthesis is recent.

# Ziermann & Diogo 2019 — Development of head muscles in fishes + phylogeny-ontogeny links

## Citation

Ziermann, J.M. & Diogo, R. 2019. Development of Head Muscles in Fishes and Notes on Phylogeny-Ontogeny Links: A Basis for Evo-Devo and Developmental Research on Fish Muscles. Chapter 10 in: Johanson, Z. et al. (eds.), *Evolution and Development of Fishes*. Cambridge University Press. DOI 10.1017/9781316832172.011. Published online December 2018.

## Format note

Synthetic book chapter (Cambridge University Press), with three principal deliverables — (1) comparative head-muscle inventory + LCA reconstructions for gnathostomes, chondrichthyans, and osteichthyans; (2) a phylo-ontogenetic developmental survey (shark *Scyliorhinus*, bichir *Polypterus*, zebrafish *Danio*, with notes on gar *Lepisosteus*, lungfish *Neoceratodus*); (3) a molecular-developmental section linking the cardiopharyngeal field (CPF) to neck + branchiomeric + cardiac muscle origins. Not a new primary-data paper; it is the integrative synthesis the authors' 2014–2017 primary publications were leading toward.

## Verbatim quotes (extracted first)

**Framing + success-of-gnathostomes claim:**

> "The overwhelming majority of vertebrates are jawed gnathostomes. The success of this group is largely correlated with the evolution of the jaw and jaw muscles, as well as the evolution of head and neck muscles, that enabled the transition from filtration to active predation."

> "Jawed vertebrates, or gnathostomes, make up 99.9 per cent of vertebrates (Coates, 2009) and currently comprise nearly 60,000 species."

**Organization of head muscles by innervation + origin:**

> "The cephalic muscles comprise pharyngeal arch muscles, as the mandibular arch muscles that are associated with the jaw, hyoid arch muscles that are related to the hyoid apparatus, and more posterior branchial arch muscles, as well as hypobranchial and epibranchial muscles, which are somite-derived."

> "The branchiomeric muscles are, from anterior to posterior: extraocular muscles related to the eye and innervated by three cranial nerves (CNIII, oculomotor; CNIV, trochlear; CNVI, abducens); mandibular (1st arch) muscles are related to the jaw and are innervated by the trigeminal nerve (CNV); hyoid (2nd arch) muscles are related to the hyoid apparatus and are innervated by the facial nerve (CNVII); and branchial (3rd and more posterior arches) muscles are innervated by the glossopharyngeal (CNIX), vagus (CNX), or spinal accessory (CNXI)."

> "Two groups of muscles originate from somites and then migrate to the head region, thus joining near the branchiomeric muscles: epibranchial muscles are mainly dorsal muscles innervated by spinal accessorii (CNXI) and/or spinal nerves and only present in adult specimens of cartilaginous fishes, and hypobranchial muscles are innervated by hypoglossal (CNXII) and/or spinal nerves and are present in all major vertebrate groups, including infrahyoid and tongue muscles."

**Plesiomorphy of chondrichthyan muscles:**

> "It has been empirically shown that despite having some derived (apomorphic) features, chondrichthyans such as sharks and skates have retained more plesiomorphic gnathostome muscle features than other living gnathostomes."

**LCA reconstructions (the numerical core of the chapter):**

> "Ziermann et al. (2014) reasoned that the last common ancestor (LCA) of extant gnathostomes had at least 5 mandibular muscles (intermandibularis anterior, intermandibularis posterior, adductor mandibulae A2, levator arcus palatini, and 1 or more labial muscles, i.e., preorbitalis), 3 hyoid muscles (interhyoideus, adductor arcus palatini, constrictor hyoideus dorsalis), 6 true branchial muscles (cucullaris, 5 constrictores branchiales, and perhaps also adductores branchiales and/or interbranchiales), 5 ventral branchial muscles (5 or more coracobranchiales), 1 or more epibranchial muscles, and 2 hypobranchial muscles (coracomandibularis, sternohyoideus)."

> "Only a few features changed from the LCA of gnathostomes to the inferred condition in the LCA of extant chondrichthyans (Ziermann et al., 2014). Mandibular and hyoid muscles did not change, but the number of branchial, epibranchial, and hypobranchial muscles increased: 13 true branchial muscles..."

> "More changes are inferred to the LCA of extant osteichthyans: 4 adductor mandibulae (A2, A3', A3'', and Aω), as well as, a labial muscle might be present... The hyoid muscles did not change in osteichthyans, but major changes occurred in the evolution of branchial muscles: the cucullaris gave rise to the protractor pectoralis and 5 levatores arcuum branchialium; only 5 constrictores branchiales are present (adductores branchiales and interbranchiales are absent); ventral branchial muscles diversified substantially... Furthermore, laryngeal muscles appear first in osteichthyans (1 constrictor laryngis, perhaps dilatator laryngis) and epibranchial muscles are absent in adult bony fishes, while the hypobranchial muscles did not change."

**Cucullaris + neck evolution (the Theis 2010 / Diogo 2015b / Sefton 2016 stepwise model):**

> "Neck evolution was a stepwise macroevolutionary event, where initially the undivided cucullaris was attached to both the pectoral girdle and branchial arches, as in sharks (e.g., *Squalus*; Ziermann et al., 2014), or split in several bundles (holocephalans, batoids)... This was followed, in osteichthyans, by the division of the muscle into the levatores arcuum branchialium, which attach to the branchial arches, and the protractor pectoralis, which attaches to the pectoral girdle. Finally, in tetrapods the protractor pectoralis differentiated into various muscles (e.g., trapezius, sternocleidomastoideus)."

> "Interestingly, in at least some members of the fossil placoderms the cucullaris might not have been attached to the pectoral girdle (Trinajstic et al., 2013), which would support the branchial arch derivation of the cucullaris (Ziermann et al., 2014)."

> "A recent clonal study has conclusively shown, after centuries of discussion on the embryological origin of this muscle, that it is truly a branchiomeric muscle derived from the cardiopharyngeal field (Lescroart et al., 2015)."

**Developmental pattern (the anterior-to-posterior, outside-in, origin-to-insertion rule + its exceptions):**

> "Those studies describe the development of head muscles as progressing, in general, from anterior to posterior, from lateral/superficial to ventral/medial (outside-in), and from origin to insertion."

> "In S. canicula, the constrictor dorsalis is the only visible muscle anlage of the mandibular arch in stage 28, while the hyoid arch muscle anlage has already differentiated into the adductor arcus palatini and constrictor hyoideus dorsalis... This shark/basal actinopterygian correspondence could indicate that the plesiomorphic developmental pattern in gnathostomes includes mandibular muscles developing after the hyoid muscles. If the same pattern was also found in other chondrichthyans, this could support Miyashita's (2015) idea that the mandibular arch was originally not integrated with, or similar to, the ancestral pharyngeal arches and only became secondarily integrated with, and similar to, these in the transitions that lead to the LCA of gnathostomes."

> "Most muscles in S. canicula develop from their region of origin to their region of insertion, except for the coracomandibularis, coracoarcualis, and coracobranchiales, which develop from their region of insertion (mandible, ceratohyal, and branchial arches, respectively) to their origin at the pectoral girdle."

**Phylo-ontogeny parallelism (the Haeckelian-style correspondence, with exceptions flagged):**

> "Comparing the developmental appearance of cephalic muscles in zebrafish (Table 10.3) with the evolutionary origin of those muscles, Diogo et al. (2008b) found that, in general, a parallelism exists between both sequences. Most of the mandibular muscles (intermandibularis anterior, intermandibularis posterior, adductor mandibulae, and levator arcus palatini) develop early and were seemingly present in basal osteichthyans."

> "While there is in general a correspondence between the ontogeny and the phylogenetic appearance of muscles in osteichthyans, some further exceptions (heterochrony) can be noted. For example, the levator arcuum branchialium V develops early, and in the 4-day-old zebrafish larvae is already much larger than other branchial muscles... Evolutionary, by contrast, hypertrophy of the levator arcuum branchialium V did not occur until the cypriniforms, which is much later than the division of the adductor mandibulae."

**Molecular section + cardiopharyngeal field:**

> "In numerous gnathostomes, the mandibular muscle plate gives rise to a dorsal premyogenic condensation called the constrictor dorsalis, a medial condensation called the adductor mandibulae and a ventral condensation named the intermandibularis (Edgeworth, 1935). In teleosts, the expression of *engrailed* marks cells dorsally located in the first arch (mandibular arch) indicating the presence of a constrictor dorsalis in these fish (Hatta et al., 1990, 1991) and enabling the comparison of this muscle in other fishes."

> "In gene knockdown experiments, specimens have both jaw- and mandibular-muscle defects, where the defect in one causes the defect in the other (Schilling et al., 1996; Heude et al., 2010; Hinits et al., 2011). For example, the dorsoventrally nested expression of *Dlx* genes in the neural crest is needed for a proper development of the jaw skeleton in gnathostomes and also for associated muscles (Depew et al., 2002; Medeiros and Crump, 2012; Gillis et al., 2013). Specifically, mice with inactivated *Dlx5* and *Dlx6* have jaw defects and reduced or missing jaw muscles (Heude et al., 2010). Importantly, the muscle defects are not caused by a loss of mandibular identity because the muscles are still present in *EndRA*-/- (Endothelin Receptor Type A) mutants that show similar jaw malformations."

> "Those upstream factors are *Pitx2* and *Tbx1* for branchial muscles and *Pax3* for trunk muscles... In *Pax3;Myf5* double-mutant mice, all somite-derived muscles (including some tongue and infrahyoid muscles) are missing, but the trapezius and sternocleidomastoideus are present, as are other head and branchial muscles (Tajbakhsh et al., 1997). In *Tbx1* mutant mice, the cucullaris derivatives are absent, as are other branchial muscles, but somite-derived limb muscles are unaffected (Theis et al., 2010)."

> "This common mesodermal progenitor field is the cardiopharyngeal field (CPF), comprising the anterior lateral mesoderm of the first heart field (FHF) and the pharyngeal mesoderm. The latter gives rise to the second heart field (SHF), which differentiates into specific heart regions and branchiomeric muscles (Diogo et al., 2015a)."

> "Interestingly, it was also shown that some branchiomeric muscle groups of one side of the body are more closely related to the same side of the heart than to the same muscle group of the other side."

> "With respect to the cucullaris and its derivatives (sternocleidomastoideus, trapezius), it was shown that they develop from *Pax3-;Mesp1+;Tbx1+* progenitor cells, identifying it as a branchiomeric muscle derived from the posterior SHF, and that the left neck muscles are clonally related to the left myocardium (pulmonary trunk, left atrium, left superior caval vein, and pulmonary vein), while the right neck muscles are clonally related to the right myocardium (right atrium, right superior caval vein)."

**Medical relevance / syndrome connection:**

> "The DiGeorge Syndrome (Del22q11.2 syndrome), for example, is caused by a microdeletion in the gene encoding Tbx1, leading to a range of craniofacial and cardiovascular anomalies. Understanding the mechanisms that generate specific phenotypes (normal and pathological) will shed light on important questions: Why do many myopathies effect only a specific subset of muscles? Are aetiologies linked to the embryonic (and evolutionary) history of the muscles? Are craniofacial myological abnormalities linked to specific cardiac defects?"

## Question

Three coupled questions. (1) What is the plesiomorphic head-muscle configuration of the gnathostome LCA, and how does it transform stepwise to the chondrichthyan LCA and the osteichthyan LCA? (2) Does the developmental sequence of head-muscle differentiation in extant fish embryos (and larvae) parallel the evolutionary sequence of muscle appearance in the phylogenetic record? (3) How does the molecular-genetic evidence from the cardiopharyngeal field (CPF) — the *Mesp1+ / Tbx1+ / Lhx2+ / Isl1+* cell population shared by the second heart field and branchiomeric muscle progenitors — constrain our interpretation of cephalic-muscle homology, neck evolution, and human craniofacial-cardiac syndromes?

## Time frame

Extant. Fossil anchoring only via Trinajstic et al. 2013 (Gogo Formation placoderm muscle + soft tissue preservation) for cucullaris plesiomorphy.

## Methods

- **Comparative-anatomy synthesis**: the LCA reconstructions are cladistic character-polarisation exercises (Ziermann et al. 2014 + Diogo & Abdala 2010) over dissected + literature-described taxa, outgrouped to cyclostomes.
- **Developmental staging**: MyoD and myosin heavy-chain antibody immunohistochemistry on cleared whole-mount embryos (Ziermann et al. 2017b *S. canicula*; Noda et al. 2017 *P. senegalus*; Diogo et al. 2008b + Schilling & Kimmel 1997 *D. rerio*). Tables 10.1–10.3 tabulate muscle presence/absence (x = present regardless of differentiation status; o = absent or unstained) across embryonic + larval stages.
- **Molecular summary** (from the literature): *engrailed* expression marks constrictor dorsalis (Hatta et al. 1990, 1991); *Dlx5/6* knockout mouse phenotype (Heude et al. 2010); *Pax3;Myf5* double mutant (Tajbakhsh et al. 1997); *Tbx1* mutant (Theis et al. 2010); clonal cell-lineage fate-mapping of mouse CPF (Lescroart et al. 2010, 2015).
- No new primary anatomical data in the chapter itself — it synthesizes the authors' 2014–2017 publication series + collaborators' work.

## Findings (numbered)

1. **Head-muscle inventory of the gnathostome LCA (Ziermann et al. 2014, re-stated)**: at least 5 mandibular muscles (intermandibularis anterior, intermandibularis posterior, adductor mandibulae A2, levator arcus palatini, ≥1 labial/preorbitalis), 3 hyoid muscles (interhyoideus, adductor arcus palatini, constrictor hyoideus dorsalis), 6 true branchial muscles (cucullaris + 5 constrictores branchiales, ± adductores/interbranchiales), ≥5 ventral branchial (coracobranchiales), ≥1 epibranchial, 2 hypobranchial (coracomandibularis, sternohyoideus).
2. **Only the branchial, epibranchial, and hypobranchial muscles changed between the gnathostome LCA and the chondrichthyan LCA**; mandibular + hyoid muscles are conserved. Chondrichthyans added 13 true branchial, 5 ventral-branchial-subtypes, 2 epibranchial, 3 hypobranchial muscles.
3. **Bigger changes on the osteichthyan stem**: 4 adductor mandibulae subdivisions (A2, A3', A3'', Aω) emerge; cucullaris splits into protractor pectoralis + 5 levatores arcuum branchialium; adductores + interbranchiales are lost; ventral-branchial diversifies; **laryngeal muscles appear first in osteichthyans**; epibranchial muscles are lost as adult structures in bony fishes.
4. **Cucullaris / neck stepwise model**: undivided shark-like cucullaris (attached to both pectoral girdle + branchial arches, though holocephalans + batoids have it split into bundles) → osteichthyan division into levatores arcuum branchialium + protractor pectoralis → tetrapod differentiation of protractor pectoralis into trapezius + sternocleidomastoideus. Trinajstic et al. 2013's placoderm evidence supports a branchial-arch (not girdle) origin.
5. **Cucullaris is a true branchiomeric muscle** — Lescroart et al. 2015 clonal analysis closed a century-long debate; the muscle derives from the cardiopharyngeal field (posterior second heart field).
6. **Developmental-sequence rule + its one systematic exception**: head muscles develop anterior-to-posterior, lateral/superficial-to-ventral/medial (outside-in), origin-to-insertion — except the coracomandibularis, coracoarcualis, and coracobranchiales in *S. canicula*, which develop in the opposite direction (insertion-to-origin) because their origin-cartilages (pectoral girdle) ossify later than their insertion sites.
7. **Mandibular muscles develop AFTER hyoid muscles in *Scyliorhinus* and *Polypterus*** — the plesiomorphic gnathostome pattern. Zebrafish shows the opposite (mandibular before hyoid), which is a derived teleost state consistent with Miyashita 2015's proposal that the mandibular arch was ancestrally distinct from the more posterior pharyngeal arches and became "pharyngeal" only on the gnathostome stem.
8. **Phylo-ontogenetic parallelism holds in most osteichthyans** (e.g., mandibular muscles present in basal osteichthyans develop early; levator opercula + adductor arcus palatini appear late in both ontogeny + phylogeny) **with clear heterochronic exceptions** — e.g., levator arcuum branchialium V is hypertrophied in *Danio* larvae from day 4, but this muscle only hypertrophied evolutionarily within Cypriniformes; here the ontogenetic timing predates the phylogenetic innovation.
9. **Cardiopharyngeal field (CPF) is the shared progenitor population**: *Mesp1+* pan-CPF progenitors give rise to FHF (left ventricle, atrial portions) + Tbx1+ progenitors for SHF (outflow + right ventricle) + branchiomeric head/neck muscles. *Tbx1* mutant mice lose cucullaris derivatives + other branchial muscles but not somite-derived limb muscles. *Pax3;Myf5* double-mutant mice lose somite-derived muscles (tongue, infrahyoid, limb) but keep trapezius + sternocleidomastoideus + other head/branchial muscles — direct developmental dissociation of the two mesodermal populations.
10. **Left-right cardiac-neck clonal coupling (Lescroart et al. 2015)**: trapezius + sternocleidomastoideus on one side share clonal origin with the myocardium of the **same** side (pulmonary trunk + left atrium + left superior caval vein for the left; right atrium + right superior caval vein for the right), not with the contralateral homologous muscle. This is a surprising developmental result with no anatomical clue.
11. **Neck is a transition zone** where branchiomeric muscles (e.g., cucullaris derivatives) overlap with somite-derived muscles (e.g., neck extensors in mammals). The splenius muscle in mice has contributions from both mesodermal populations (Lescroart et al. 2015).
12. **Medical payoff — DiGeorge syndrome**: 22q11.2 microdeletion → haploinsufficiency for *Tbx1* → craniofacial + cardiovascular anomalies, understood here as a single developmental failure in the CPF that links the two organ systems. Frames three open questions for the clinical community: why do myopathies hit specific muscle subsets, are etiologies tied to embryonic history, and are craniofacial myological abnormalities linked to specific cardiac defects?

## Limitations

- Not a primary-data paper; anchored entirely on the authors' own Ziermann et al. 2014 + 2017b + Noda et al. 2017 + Diogo et al. 2008b data sets. Reader who wants original sections + stain protocols must pull those.
- Only three taxa developmentally staged in detail (*Scyliorhinus*, *Polypterus*, *Danio*); the "plesiomorphic gnathostome developmental pattern" claim (mandibular after hyoid) rests on two non-teleost taxa, neither of which is a holocephalan or batoid, so the chondrichthyan baseline is still a single shark species. Authors explicitly flag this: "clearly more fishes need to be analysed, including a detailed description of out-group species like hagfishes and lampreys (cyclostomes)."
- Some muscle identifications remain contested (e.g., mandibulohyoideus of *Hydrolagus* is CNVII-innervated in Ziermann et al. 2014 but Anderson 2008 hypothesises CNIX + CNVII — open question in the chapter); the chapter cites both without resolving.
- The "phylo-ontogenetic parallelism" is Haeckelian in shape and has explicit exceptions (levator arcuum branchialium V in *Danio*; see Finding 8). Authors note "barring a few exceptions" without attempting to quantify how many exceptions would falsify the parallelism rule.
- Molecular section relies heavily on mouse mutant phenotypes (*Tbx1*, *Pax3;Myf5*, *Dlx5/6*) — none performed in fish. The linking inference to fish cephalic-muscle development is by homology-argument from gene expression patterns, not by fish mutant screens.
- Kryukova 2017 subdivision of shark hyoid + branchial muscles in *Heptranchias perlo* is acknowledged + partially dismissed ("some of those divisions seem to be arbitrary and do not match with what other authors have previously described in this species") without a figure-by-figure rebuttal.
- Placoderm evidence (Trinajstic et al. 2013) is invoked qualitatively; the chapter does not score the placoderm muscle-anatomy states into its LCA matrix.
- No engineered-cartilage / 3D reconstruction. Reader needs to pair with Ziermann et al. 2017b (*S. canicula* MyoD sections) for primary images.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Four distinct teaching uses. (1) **As a one-stop LCA field guide for fish head muscles** — Section 10.1 gives the numerical inventory (5 mandibular + 3 hyoid + 6 branchial…) that you can put on a slide and poll the class to name each muscle. (2) **As the phylo-ontogeny primer** — Tables 10.1–10.3 + Fig. 10.2 let students trace a single muscle across three species and three developmental stages without needing to master three separate staging systems. Use Tables 10.1–10.3 directly as a class exercise: "which muscle appears first in *Scyliorhinus*? in *Polypterus*? in *Danio*? what does that tell us about the gnathostome LCA?" (3) **As the molecular-developmental synthesis** — Section 10.3.2 + Fig. 10.3 introduce the cardiopharyngeal field framework for upper-level students, connecting head-muscle embryology to the heart (which is a revelation for most students) and motivating a diagnostic case (DiGeorge syndrome) that ties the comparative anatomy to a clinical outcome they can explain back. (4) **As the evo-devo teaching paper that situates the cucullaris** — the neck evolution stepwise model here is more complete than in Diogo & Ziermann 2015a, 2015b (which only treats the adult state). Pair with Diogo & Ziermann 2015 paired-appendage paper (already in library; same authors, overlapping neck-muscle content with forelimb-hindlimb serial homology), Ziermann, Miyashita & Diogo 2014 (already in library, the primary LCA-reconstruction paper), Anderson 2008 (already in library, cranial muscle homology), Diogo 2008 + Diogo & Abdala 2010 (already in library, osteichthyan + sarcopterygian coverage), Heude et al. 2010 + Theis et al. 2010 + Lescroart et al. 2015 (mouse mutants + clonal analyses cited here, not in library but worth linking to), Miyashita 2015 (mandibular-arch-distinctness hypothesis), Trinajstic et al. 2015 *Biol. Rev.* placoderm musculature (in library, upcoming), Crompton et al. 2018 (mammaliaform braincase, pair for the head-muscle → mammalian neck-muscle transition), Castiello & Brazeau 2018 (placoderm braincase μCT), Brazeau & Friedman 2014 (placoderm paraphyly + early-gnathostome character hierarchy). **Pedagogic headline**: the head is a zone of shared embryonic origin with the heart, and that single fact reorganizes how students should think about cranial-nerve-innervation, branchiomeric vs somitic muscles, and the evolution of the neck.

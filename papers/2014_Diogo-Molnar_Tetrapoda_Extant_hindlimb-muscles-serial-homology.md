# Diogo & Molnar 2014 — Tetrapod hindlimb muscles and deconstruction of FL–HL serial homology

## Citation

Diogo R, Molnar J. 2014. Comparative anatomy, evolution, and homologies of tetrapod hindlimb muscles, comparison with forelimb muscles, and deconstruction of the forelimb–hindlimb serial homology hypothesis. *The Anatomical Record* 297: 1047–1075. DOI 10.1002/ar.22919.

## Question

"Why are the pelvic and pectoral appendages of gnathostomes generally so similar to each other?" Is the similarity the result of ancestral duplication (serial homology in the strict sense) or of evolutionary parallelism / convergence through shared developmental modules? The authors explicitly set out to test the ≈240-year-old serial homology hypothesis of Vicq d'Azyr 1774, Oken 1843, and Owen 1849, which has "been accepted without serious question" and is still in most textbooks.

## Taxa and material

First-hand dissections by the authors and colleagues of:

- *Ambystoma mexicanum* (axolotl) — Urodela
- *Xenopus laevis* — Anura
- *Trachemys scripta* — Testudines
- *Timon lepidus* (ocellated lizard) — Squamata, Lepidosauria
- *Alligator mississippiensis* — Crocodylia
- *Gallus gallus* — Aves
- *Rattus norvegicus* — Mammalia, Euarchontoglires
- *Homo sapiens* — Mammalia, Euarchontoglires

Rat chosen as "anatomically generalized" therian and as primate-related outgroup comparator. Hundreds of specimens across vertebrates already dissected by Diogo and colleagues in preceding monographs (Diogo & Abdala 2010; Diogo & Tanaka 2012; Diogo et al. 2013) supply the comparative backdrop.

## Time frame

Extant, but with explicit reference to Paleozoic stem gnathostomes (the antiarch placoderm *Parayunnanolepis* ≈413 Mya, Zhu et al. 2012) as the fossil anchor showing that PEC–PEL appendages are markedly dissimilar in plesiomorphic condition. So really: "extant + deep-time FL/HL comparative evolution."

## Methods

1. **Methodology for "correspondence"** per Diogo et al. 2013. Three criteria used for topological correspondence between FL and HL muscles: (a) distal attachments, (b) proximal attachments, (c) orientation of fibers / number of divisions / joints crossed / relations to other hard- and soft-tissue structures. Developmental, genetic, innervation, and gene-expression data layered on top but **topological correspondence is the primary screen** — "the simplest, most conservative and thus least subjective method available to compare these muscles."

2. **Four nested tables per taxon** (Tables 1–12). For each of the eight focal taxa, the authors tabulate: (1) pelvic + thigh muscles, (2) dorsal / extensor leg muscles, (3) ventral / flexor leg muscles, (4) foot muscles. Arrows in the table indicate alternative homology hypotheses supported by different authors; bolded arrows = strongest evidence. Extensive footnotes review the century-long history of each named muscle.

3. **FL–HL "topological correspondence" summary tables** (Tables 13–21). For each taxon, every HL muscle with a direct one-to-one FL "topological equivalent" is listed. Non-topological data (ontogeny, gene expression, transgenic phenotypes) discussed as separate threads so the user can see which hypotheses rest on which type of evidence.

4. **Unifying nomenclature** proposed (Fig. 2, Tables 1–21). Homologous muscles get the same name across taxa; names already in human Terminologia Anatomica (1998) and Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria (2005) retained where possible. Where the human and anuran (say) muscle named "gluteus maximus" derive from different ancestral anlagen, the authors use the anuran-specific name (e.g., "extensor iliotibialis B") to avoid false-positive homology claims from shared names.

## Findings

1. **FL–HL muscle "correspondence" counts scale with derivedness, not with plesiomorphy** (Fig. 2). The number of HL muscles or muscle-groups with a direct FL "topological equivalent":
   - Basal fossil gnathostome (antiarch placoderm *Parayunnanolepis*, 413 Mya): 0 — PEC and PEL "not anatomically similar" at all.
   - Chondrichthyans: 2 (abductor + adductor of each fin).
   - Dipnoans (closest living tetrapod outgroup): 2 — "remarkably different" girdles + muscles.
   - Plesiomorphic tetrapod *Ambystoma*: 19 correspondences — autopodium + zeugopodium strikingly similar; girdles + proximal muscles different.
   - Anurans *Xenopus*: 12.
   - Turtles *Trachemys*: 13; crocodilians *Alligator*: 14; birds *Gallus*: 9.
   - Lepidosaurs *Timon*: 14; rodents *Rattus*: 16.
   - Humans: 19 — tied with salamanders for the highest count.
   
   This inverts the classical expectation. If serial homology were real, plesiomorphic fishes with paired appendages should show the **highest** FL–HL muscle correspondence, not the lowest. Instead, similarity accumulates in derived tetrapods — the signature of convergence, not ancestral duplication.

2. **The "similarity bottleneck" topology.** Paired appendages originated as distinct (Zhu et al. 2012 placoderm), diverged anatomically along each fish lineage (cf. dipnoan PEC vs. PEL girdles), then **re-converged** toward similar muscle complements in derived tetrapods via shared developmental modules being recruited into each limb. Fig. 2 is the single clearest visual summary. "The evolutionary history of the pelvic and pectoral appendages is much more complex than the 'serial homology followed by functional/anatomical divergence' hypothesis suggests."

3. **Shared genes ≠ shared morphological homology.** The *Tbx4*/*Tbx5* paralogy that patterns HL and FL respectively resulted from a gene duplication, but the morphological appendages are not therefore duplications of a single ancestral appendage. "That similar genes contribute to an outgrowth that gives rise to different limbs in the same animal does not necessarily mean that these limbs are serial homologues; similar genes and gene cascades/networks may have been simply recruited homoplastically as organizers of limb development."

4. **Specific FL–HL parallelism cases from the tables.** Table 13 example: in rats, short extensors of digits 2 and 3 are fused into an undivided muscle in both limbs — extensor indicis in the FL, extensor digitorum brevis in the HL. These fusions are **independently derived** in the two limbs, producing apparent "serial" similarity by parallelism. Table 14 example: HL popliteus + FL pronator teres ("topological equivalents") actually derive from different anlagen — popliteus from interosseus cruris, pronator teres from flexor antebrachii et carpi radialis; HL tibialis posterior and FL palmaris profundus / pronator profundus are the real ontogenetic counterparts. The topological-equivalence claim is **secondary** to the developmental-anlage claim.

5. **Anuran pelvic + thigh muscle nomenclature is full of false-friend names** (Table 5). "Tensor fasciae latae," "gluteus maximus," "gemellus," "obturator externus," "quadratus femoris," "obturator internus," "pectineus," "adductor longus," "iliacus," "sartorius," "semitendinosus," "adductor magnus" — none of these anuran muscles are homologous to the human muscles of the same name. The last common ancestor of Lissamphibia + Amniota had none of them. Names shared with human anatomy reflect cross-linguistic naming convenience, not homology. The authors switch to Prikryl et al. 2009 letter codes ("pubioischiofemoralis internus A/B/C/D") to remove the confusion.

6. **Specific homology calls that overturn common textbook claims:**
   - The salamander "ulnar condyle" of Francis 1934 is actually the **entepicondyle** (the authors follow Sigurdsen & Bolt 2009 on this — cf. 2009_Sigurdsen-Bolt note).
   - The reptilian "iliotibialis," "ambiens," and "puboischiofemoralis externus" of Ashley-Ross 1992 are mutually inconsistent across reptile clades — the mammalian sartorius derives from the **anterior head of puboischiofemoralis internus** (via Walker & Homberger 1997), not from any "ambiens."
   - Mammalian tenuissimus is not homologous to the "caudal head of the biceps femoris" (contra Gregory & Camp 1918, Romer 1922, Haines 1935); it is a dorsally derived muscle from the tail region, homologous with the reptilian "flexor cruris tarsi dorsalis" (via Jones 1979).
   - Mammalian gracilis has a single head, anuran gracilis has two ("gracilis major" + "gracilis minor") — both ancestral to the gracilis of tetrapods but via different pubioischiotibialis heads.
   - Fibularis tertius of humans is **not** homologous to the fibularis digiti quinti of rats — humans derive fibularis tertius from the extensor digitorum longus anlage (Lewis 1910); rat fibularis digiti quinti derives from the extensor digiti quinti anlage.

7. **Conclusion: FL–HL serial homology is rejected in the strict sense.** FL–HL similarity is explained by "evolutionary parallelism/convergence due to a complex interplay of ontogenetic, functional, topological, and phylogenetic constraints/factors." What Owen 1849 actually called "serial homology" in *On the Nature of Limbs* was largely "parallelism" in modern usage — the strict ancestral-duplication reading entered the textbooks later.

## Key figures and tables

- **Fig. 1** — the textbook "serial homology followed by functional/anatomical divergence" hypothesis as a trapezoid: hypothetical ancestral fish with identical PEC + PEL at the bottom widens into more distinct human limbs at the top. This is the hypothesis the paper is going to deconstruct. Use as the "this is what we're testing" slide.
- **Fig. 2** — the revised picture. An inverted-hourglass: *Parayunnanolepis* (0 correspondences) → chondrichthyans (2) → dipnoans (2) → *Ambystoma* (19) → *Xenopus* (12) / turtles (13) / crocs (14) / birds (9) / lizards (14) / rats (16) / humans (19). Similarity is a derived condition, not a retained primitive one. **This is the single most valuable figure in the paper.**
- **Fig. 3** — superficial forelimb vs. hindlimb musculature of the salamander *Taricha torosa* in dorsal view, color-coded. Forearm-hand muscles strikingly similar to leg-foot muscles; pectoral-arm muscles clearly different from pelvic-thigh muscles. The "distal > proximal" similarity gradient is immediately visible.
- **Tables 1–12** — per-taxon homology schemes with arrows showing competing hypotheses. Table 1 (pelvic + thigh muscles of *Timon*, *Rattus*, *Homo*) and Table 2 (dorsal/extensor leg muscles of *Ambystoma*, *Timon*, *Rattus*, *Homo*) are the amniote-focused dictionaries.
- **Tables 5–6** — *Ambystoma* vs. *Xenopus* pelvic/thigh and dorsal leg-muscle correspondences. Where to go when teaching amphibian hindlimb.
- **Tables 13–21** — FL–HL "topological equivalence" matrices per taxon. The practical use is a cross-check: when a colleague claims muscle X of the HL is the "serial homologue" of muscle Y of the FL, look it up here and see whether the developmental anlage supports it.

## What Tables 13–21 settled, and what they contradicted

Tables 13–21 are the forelimb–hindlimb correspondence tables, and their captions
use the phrase this dataset's `basis: "topological"` was named for: "clear
topological correspondences". They read cleanly as page images (pp. 1068–69 of the
PDF) where the text layer collapses their columns. Reading them against the
dataset's own forelimb–hindlimb `serial` edges split those edges three ways.

**Fifteen pairs are stated outright and are now sourced to the table that states
them** — the abductor digiti minimi, contrahentes, contrahentium caput longum, both
flexores accessorii, flexores breves profundi and superficiales, intermetacarpales
and intermetatarsales, extensores digitorum breves, extensor digitorum, flexor
digitorum longus, tibialis anterior against the extensor antebrachii et carpi
radialis, the interosseus cruris against the pronator quadratus, the popliteus
against the pronator teres, and the abductor et extensor digiti 1 against the
abductor pollicis longus.

**A sixteenth, added 2026-08-20: the lumbricales.** The pair could not be scored
before because this dataset had no `lumbricales-pes`; it does now, and Table 15
carries the row. Foot lumbricales in *Timon lepidus* (2 muscles, to digits 3-4),
*Rattus norvegicus* (4, to digits 2-4) and *Homo sapiens* (4, to digits 2-5), each
against the hand lumbricales in red; **`—` for *Ambystoma mexicanum***, which is the
urodele absence the running text turns on when it declines to say whether the
lumbricales were present in the last common ancestor of tetrapods. The edge was
first scored `moderate` on the belief that Table 15's rows could not be read, which
this note had already recorded as false — the tables read cleanly as page images
where the text layer collapses them. Reading page 23 of the PDF settled it in a
minute and the edge is `well-supported` like the other fifteen.

**Nine pairs are ones this paper explicitly denies.** Every pelvic-or-thigh against
pectoral-or-arm edge in the dataset falls here. The paper's statement is flat:
across every tetrapod clade in Tables 13–21, including anatomically plesiomorphic
urodeles, *not even one pelvic-thigh muscle has a clear "topological equivalent" in
the pectoral region and arm*, a lack they attribute to phylogenetic constraint on
the girdles — the girdles were already markedly different in fish, and only the
distal limb converged. Those nine edges are kept, because the pairings are ones the
literature makes and a student will look for, but each now carries a `caution`
naming this paper as denying it. **They are deliberately left unsourced**, because
no source supports them and a citation here would read as if one did.

**Three pairs conflict with a specific pairing in Table 14** and are flagged rather
than sourced:

- `flexor-digitorum-longus` ↔ `gastrocnemius` — Table 14 gives the gastrocnemius no
  forelimb equivalent at all; the cell is blank for *Timon*, *Rattus* and *Homo*.
- `pronator-quadratus` ↔ `tibialis-posterior` — Table 14 pairs the pronator
  quadratus with the interosseus cruris, and the tibialis posterior with the
  palmaris profundus 1 or the flexor carpi radialis. Our edge crosses both, and it
  claims `basis: "developmental"`, which is a shared-anlage claim this paper does
  not support.
- `pronator-profundus-pes` ↔ `pronator-quadratus` — "pronator profundus" is this
  paper's alternative name for the *leg* muscle tibialis posterior. Whether our foot
  record is that muscle needs settling before the edge can be attributed.

The `no-counterpart` edges are the other side of the same result and are sourced
here: five records assert no forelimb counterpart, and this paper's general finding
is exactly that.

One thing in the tables the dataset does not yet carry: the captions state that
where a forelimb "equivalent" **clearly has a different ontogeny, it is set in
italics.** That is a per-pair anlage-mismatch flag, already published, and it maps
onto `caution` under `basis: "developmental"`. Reading the italics off the plates
is a discrete follow-up pass.

## Limitations

Authors are explicit on several points:

- **"Topological correspondence" is deliberately chosen as the conservative screen** because full developmental + genetic data do not exist for most of the taxa. Where they do exist (e.g., Cihak 1972 on human hand/foot, Dunlap 1966 on anuran ontogeny) the topological call sometimes holds up and sometimes does not (the Cihak 1972 conclusion that human FL and HL are "not homologous tissues — they are homodynamic structures" is quoted approvingly).
- **No new developmental, genetic, or transgenic work** — the paper is a synthesis of dissection + literature review. Its main product is a cross-clade muscle-name dictionary and a reinterpretation of existing evidence.
- **Sampling in non-amniote tetrapods is thin** — one urodele (*Ambystoma*), one anuran (*Xenopus*). Caecilians are excluded (limbless). Across Squamata, only *Timon*; across Testudines, only *Trachemys*.
- **The "correspondence counts" in Fig. 2 depend on where you draw the line between "muscle" and "muscle group"** — Tables 13–21 show that some of the apparent correspondences are soft (e.g., gracilis — multiple heads in anurans vs. single head in mammals). A skeptic could legitimately recount the rat and human columns differently. The authors' own methodology section acknowledges this.
- **The paper does not settle where FL–HL similarity actually came from** in a mechanistic sense. It rejects ancestral-duplication but leaves open which specific developmental modules (Lbx1-mediated migration, Tbx4/Tbx5 regulation, Hox13 patterning, etc.) are responsible for the observed convergence. That is future work.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

Central paper for three teaching threads.

First, **"serial homology" as a concept in need of precision.** Most comparative anatomy textbooks use "serial homology" casually, for anything from true mesodermal-segment repetition (vertebrae, somite-derived muscles) to any "striking similarity" between two body parts. Diogo & Molnar force the distinction: true serial homology requires ancestral duplication; apparent FL–HL similarity is convergence on shared developmental modules. Pair with Wagner 1994 and Wilmer 2003 on the modern definition of homology.

Second, **"topological vs. developmental correspondence."** A muscle that looks like it is in the right place and inserts the right way (topological correspondence) may still not be homologous to the "equivalent" muscle if it derives from a different anlage (developmental correspondence). Table 14's popliteus / pronator teres case is the cleanest single example. Fig. 3 of this paper is the dorsal-view salamander color atlas that students should be annotating in lab.

Third, **the "similarity bottleneck" phylogenetic argument.** Students will have seen Fig. 1-type trapezoids ("fish had identical PEC/PEL → modern humans have differentiated PEC/PEL") in textbooks. Fig. 2 replaces that with the actual historical picture: PEC and PEL were **different** in stem gnathostomes, and modern tetrapod (esp. human) similarity is a derived convergence. Pair naturally with the Diogo & Ziermann 2015 chondrichthyan-focused companion (2015_Diogo-Ziermann note) which runs the same argument from the sharks/skates/chimaeras end.

Tables 1–12 are practical lecture aids when setting dissection exercises — arrows indicating "this is the ancestral state; that is the derived state" directly map to what students will see on the bench.

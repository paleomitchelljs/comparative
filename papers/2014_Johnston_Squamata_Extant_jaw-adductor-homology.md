# Johnston 2014 — lizard and snake jaw adductor homology via the folded sheet

## Citation

Johnston P. 2014. Homology of the jaw muscles in lizards and snakes — a solution from a comparative gnathostome approach. *The Anatomical Record* 297: 574–585. DOI: 10.1002/ar.22857.

## Question

Snakes arose within the lizard radiation, yet their jaw adductors look organised on a different plan. Lizards and *Sphenodon* have temporal adductors layered **lateral to medial**; macrostomatan snakes appear layered **rostral to caudal** (Haas 1973; McDowell 1986; Zaher 1994). Establishing homology, and a consistent nomenclature, had been unresolved.

## Taxa and material

New dissections across the snake tree (families marked on a cladogram modified from Wiens et al. 2012), plus literature data. *Sphenodon* is taken as close to the plesiomorphic lepidosaur condition; the iguanid *Ctenosaura pectinata* is used for illustration because the absence of a ventral temporal arch makes the comparison clearer. *Python sebae* after Frazetta (1966).

## Findings

Applying the folded-sheet model of Johnston (2011) to snakes resolves the problem, and does so in a way that vindicates McDowell (1986), whose account subsequent authors had neither adopted nor extended.

The resolution has two moves, both McDowell's:

1. **The rostral "external adductor" component of colubroid snakes is the lizard m. levator anguli oris (LAO).** In lizards the LAO is the most superficial layer and attaches to the rictal plate (Mundplatte) at the corner of the mouth. In colubroid snakes the rictal plate has become the **venom gland**, so parts or all of the LAO attach to the gland instead, or run to the mandible by fascial or direct insertion.
2. **The m. adductor mandibulae externus profundus is absent in snakes**, at least in colubroids.

With those two, lizard and snake adductors correspond. Johnston extends the reading across all snake radiations, including basal alethinophidians (less modified from the lizard condition, per Rieppel 1980) and the highly apomorphic scolecophidians.

The functional context: the snake trajectory has been toward the macrostomatan condition — wide gape to swallow whole prey, with constriction or venom rather than biting subduing it. That has **reduced adductor mass and lost aponeurotic structures in favour of longer fibres**, which is why the configuration looks so different.

## Why this matters methodologically

The apparent lizard/snake reorganisation is a change in the **folding of one sheet**, not in its composition. Components are defined by position of insertion into the inverted U, and on that criterion the two groups match. The paper is an argument that geometry of attachment, not nerve topography, is the reliable signal in this system.

## Limitations

- The homology rests on the folded-sheet model, which is Johnston's own framework from the 2011 paper rather than an independently established one.
- McDowell's model had lain unadopted for nearly thirty years; the paper revives rather than confirms it.
- Scolecophidians have highly apomorphic feeding mechanisms and specialised anatomy, so their fit is the weakest part of the extension.

## Relevance to this project

Source for the lepidosaur occurrence rows on the split adductor components, and for `levator-anguli-oris` — a muscle whose insertion target itself evolved into a novel organ. The recorded `openQuestion` on `adductor-mandibulae-longus` is McDowell's proposed loss of the profundus in colubroids.

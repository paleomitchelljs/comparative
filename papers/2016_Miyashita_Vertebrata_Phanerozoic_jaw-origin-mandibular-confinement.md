# Miyashita (2016) — Mandibular confinement and the origin of the jaw

## Citation

Miyashita T. 2016. Fishing for jaws in early vertebrate evolution: a new hypothesis
of mandibular confinement. *Biological Reviews* 91: 611–657.

## The hypothesis

More than 99% of living vertebrates have jaws, and the sequence that produced them
remains contested. The **Mandibular Confinement Hypothesis** proposes that the jaw
evolved by *spatial confinement* of the mandibular arch.

Two claims carry it:

1. **The mandibular region was initially extensive and distinct** among the
   pharyngeal arches — not simply the first of a serial set.
2. **It acquired the common pharyngeal pattern only at the origin of the jaw**,
   through a shift of a domain boundary restricting the space the mandibular arch's
   mesenchymal cells could occupy. As surrounding domains replaced mandibular
   structures at the periphery, the arch took on the pattern of its neighbours.

## What it is used for here

Cited on **`adductor-mandibulae`** as background, and it does two jobs:

- It is the framing for the mandibular-arch records generally — why arch 1 is
  treated as a segment with its own history rather than as the anterior member of a
  series.
- **It is why cyclostome feeding muscles are not homologised with the gnathostome
  adductor complex.** A lamprey's feeding apparatus occupies the mandibular
  territory without being a jaw, and equating the two would assert exactly the
  continuity this hypothesis argues against.

**No occurrence rows**, correctly — it is a review and a hypothesis, not a
description.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The best single statement of why "the jaw evolved from the first gill arch" is too
simple, and a good example of a hypothesis that changes what you are willing to call
homologous. Pair with **Ziermann et al. (2014)** for the hagfish and lamprey head as
actually described, and with **Gai et al. (2022)** for the neighbouring spiracular
segment.

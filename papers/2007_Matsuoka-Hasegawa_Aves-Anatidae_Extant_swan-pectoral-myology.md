# Matsuoka & Hasegawa (2007) — Whooper swan pectoral myology and osteology

## Citation

Matsuoka H, Hasegawa Y. 2007. Myology and osteology of the Whooper Swan *Cygnus
cygnus* (Aves: Anatidae). Part 1. Muscles attached to the sternum, coracoid,
clavicle, scapula and humerus. *Bulletin of the Gunma Museum of Natural History*
11: 7–14.

## Why it was reached for

The Aves column had one usable route into the pectoral girdle and this is the
densest source in the whole bibliography by attachment statements per page: 22
scored rows out of twelve pages.

It is also written for exactly the problem this dataset has. The authors say in
the introduction that avian myology has been described beautifully by
ornithologists — they name George & Berger (1966) — but that for a
palaeontological purpose it is still hard to identify *which structure on a bone*
carries a given muscle, tendon or ligament. Their answer is to figure and number
every attachment site. That is the same gap `data/skeleton.json` exists to close.

## The animal

One old male whooper swan, **GMNH-VA-04-02**, Gunma Museum of Natural History.
Arrived at Kiryugaoka Zoo on 3 June 1983 and died 12 August 2004, so at least 21
years in the zoo; death probably senile. Total length 1410 mm, body weight 7250 g,
wings 550 and 560 mm. The distal wing tips were not clipped and bones, tendons and
feathers were all preserved — which matters, because a clipped wing would have cost
the distal attachments.

Osteological terms follow Baumel (1979), myological terms Vanden Berge (1979),
with George & Berger's names in square brackets where they differ.

## Scored

22 occurrence rows on *Cygnus cygnus* — the pectoral girdle, the arm and seven
forearm muscles.

**Seven rows carry an origin and no insertion, and that is scope rather than
omission.** This is Part 1, which stops at the humerus: the muscles arising from
the distal humerus have their origins here and their insertions in a part that
would follow. Leaving the insertion empty reads as unrecorded, which is what it
is. One row — the biceps — is empty at the insertion for the source's own reason:
they state that it is surrounded by the proximal ulno-radial muscles and that the
attachment was not observed.

## Findings worth carrying

**Four forearm muscles arise from sharp depressions on the dorsal distal humerus**
— the avian *epicondylus dorsalis*, scored here as the lateral epicondyle. The
proximal position of that origin is a derived avian feature, and Burch (2014)
argues the ancestral archosaur condition from it.

**The supracoracoideus insertion is the flight mechanism in one sentence.** The
tendon passes through the triosseal canal to reach the dorsal humerus, which is
what converts a ventral muscle into a wing elevator. A salamander's
supracoracoideus is still a ventral adductor; the difference is the canal.

**The deltoideus heads are named the wrong way round, and they say so.** The
superficial major head is called the *posterior* head although it lies anterior to
the so-called anterior head. Recorded as the source has it, with the note.

**`M. dorsalis scapulae` is a name this dataset carries three times over.** George
& Berger use it for what is scored here as scapulohumeralis posterior; the dataset
also holds it as an old synonym of the latissimus dorsi and of the deltoideus
scapularis. Three muscles, one discarded name — the case the search index exists
to handle.

**The sternocoracoideus is the only muscle inside the thoracic cavity**, and the
authors suggest it keeps the coracosternal connection flexible.

## Limitations

- **One individual, and an old captive one.** A 21-year-old zoo swan is not a
  sample, and nothing here speaks to variation.
- **The serratus row is deliberately thin.** They record multiple muscles running
  from the vertebral column to the medial scapula, say these must be parts of Mm.
  serrati, and then say plainly they could not identify them in this study. The
  insertion area is figured; the origin is not resolved to particular vertebrae.
- **Some attachments are onto other muscles rather than onto bone.** The deep
  pectoralis layer reaches the rib cage indirectly, over the surface of underlying
  muscle, and the latissimus pars cranialis attaches aponeurotically over three
  thoracic neural spines rather than to the bone — "seems not on the bones
  directly", in their words. Both are recorded as the weaker rows they are.

## Relevance to comparative anatomy teaching

The best demonstration in the corpus of why a muscle name is not an attachment.
Every claim here is anchored to a figured, numbered point on a named bone, and the
reason the authors give for doing it that way — that you cannot reconstruct an
extinct bird otherwise — is the argument for the whole attachment model. Pair with
**Burch (2014)**, which uses avian attachment data of this kind as one half of its
bracket.
